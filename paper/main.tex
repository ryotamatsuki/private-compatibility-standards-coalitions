\title{Private Compatibility and the Stability of Standards Coalitions}

\author{Anonymous Author}

\begin{abstract}
We study when private adaptation to incompatible standards strengthens, weakens, or reverses governments' incentives for broader formal harmonization. Governments first choose among separate national standards, a two-country regional standards union, and international standardization; firms can then pay a fixed cost to support an additional formal standard. In a three-country Cournot microfoundation, a regional member's initial payoff advantage over international standardization can be written as an exclusion component minus a reciprocal disadvantage. When the outsider adopts the bloc standard but retains residual marginal adaptation cost $d$, the post-adoption payoff difference becomes $-\mathscr D+R(d,v)$, where $R(d,v)$ is an equilibrium-derived residual-rent term that is increasing in $d$. This gives exact regions in which private adaptation reverses the government's ranking, strengthens the incentive for international standardization without reversing it, or weakens that incentive. The reversal survives incomplete adaptation on a nonempty open set together with a selection-free outsider-only adoption equilibrium. The result is not generally portable: the initial regional advantage disappears at zero network effects under the canonical specification, under one alternative singleton-network specification, and throughout a pre-specified differentiated-demand comparison in both Cournot and Bertrand competition. As an institutional application, full bypass can make international standardization uniquely stable for an intermediate fixed-cost range, while the high-cost regional stable set is sensitive to the blocking rule and symmetry. The paper therefore characterizes a conditional mechanism and its failure boundaries rather than a general tendency for private compatibility to promote formal harmonization.
\end{abstract}
