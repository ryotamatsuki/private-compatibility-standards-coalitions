\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asks how firms' ability to circumvent standards incompatibility privately affects the stability of formal standards coalitions. The key distinction is between formal membership and effective compatibility. Governments choose a formal partition, but firms can subsequently support additional standards without changing that partition. Private compatibility can therefore alter the continuation payoff attached to a formal standards arrangement even when institutional membership itself is unchanged.

The central mechanism is selective erosion. A regional standards union can benefit its members by excluding the outsider in coalition-member markets while leaving those members disadvantaged in the outsider market. When the outsider alone supports the bloc standard, the member-market exclusion component disappears but the reciprocal outsider-market disadvantage remains. This asymmetric change can reverse coalition members' ranking between regional and international standardization. Private compatibility is therefore not important here because it is a new technology; its role is to change the product-market rents that sustain a previously chosen formal coalition.

Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability} shows that this continuation-payoff reversal can change formal coalition stability. On a nonempty open set of product-market parameters, high private-compatibility costs leave the three symmetric regional standards unions stable. For a nondegenerate intermediate range of compatibility costs, outsider-only bypass instead makes every regional union strictly blockable by the grand coalition and makes international standardization uniquely stable under the maintained strict-blocking concept. The result is not a claim of monotonicity over all values of $F$: sufficiently low costs can generate broader multistandarding patterns outside the headline comparison. Market-size asymmetry is likewise not needed for the stability reversal; its role in the secondary analysis is to rank potential regional partners.

The broader implication is that private technological adaptation and public harmonization need not be political substitutes even when private compatibility reduces the technological consequences of incompatible standards. Changes in the cost of multi-standard production, certification, redesign, or interoperability can redistribute the rents associated with formal arrangements and thereby change governments' incentives over regional versus broader harmonization. The paper characterizes coalition stability rather than global social optimality, so the stability of international standardization should not be read as a world-welfare ranking.

The formal result is deliberately tied to a parsimonious three-country Cournot environment with binary compatibility adoption, an exogenous fixed adoption cost, and strict blocking without transfers. Richer coalition networks, differentiated competition, bargaining, and endogenous compatibility investment may change adoption incentives or admissible deviations and therefore require separate analysis. Those are robustness and extension questions rather than conclusions established here. Within the present framework, the main lesson is narrower and sharper: post-formation firm behavior can change the continuation payoffs that determine which formal standards arrangements governments are willing to sustain.
