\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asks how firms' ability to circumvent standards incompatibility privately affects the stability of formal standards coalitions. The answer is that private compatibility can matter politically even when it leaves formal coalition membership unchanged. Firms choose whether to support additional standards after governments select a formal partition, and governments evaluate the continuation outcomes generated by those private responses. A private compatibility investment can therefore change the economic value attached to a formal institutional boundary. In the model, this feedback links a standard industrial-organization margin---firms' endogenous compatibility choices and the resulting Cournot competition---to governments' incentives over regional and international standardization.

The central mechanism is selective erosion. Without private bypass, a member of a regional standards union receives a net exclusion surplus in coalition-member markets but bears a reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market. When the outsider alone adopts the bloc standard, the member-market component disappears: the outsider is no longer excluded in coalition markets, so the member's consumer-surplus and domestic-firm profit blocks there move to their complete-compatibility counterparts. The coalition member's disadvantage in the outsider market, however, remains because the member has not adopted the outsider's standard. Private adaptation therefore does not reproduce international standardization or alter the formal partition directly; it selectively removes the payoff component that supported regional exclusion. In the parameter region identified by the model, private compatibility can consequently substitute technologically for part of the market-access role of formal harmonization while acting as a political complement in the narrower sense of increasing governments' relative incentive for international standardization.

Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability} translates this continuation-payoff mechanism into formal coalition stability. For every $(c,v)\in\Omega_0$, when the compatibility fixed cost is high, $F>2T_A$, no private adoption occurs under regional or separate standards and the strict-blocking stable set contains all three symmetric regional standards unions. When the fixed cost instead lies in the intermediate region $F_L<F<2T_A$, the outsider to each regional union adopts the bloc standard while the members do not reciprocate. This outsider-only bypass removes the member-specific advantage that supported the regional arrangement, while the outsider also strictly prefers international standardization. The grand coalition then strictly blocks every regional standards union, and international standardization is uniquely stable under the paper's maintained coalition-stability concept. The reversal occurs on an open set of product-market parameters together with a nondegenerate interval of compatibility costs; it is not a threshold-equality result. Nor does the paper infer monotonicity over all values of $F$: lower-cost multistandarding patterns lie outside this headline comparison.

The secondary market-size result sharpens partner identity without generating the Main mechanism. Proposition~\ref{prop:market-size-partner-selection} shows that, conditional on no private adoption, a government prefers the larger of two otherwise symmetric potential regional partners because moving market mass from outsider treatment to coalition-member treatment is worth $A-C=T_U>0$. Under $m_1=m_2=1$ and $m_3=1-\delta$, the two larger countries therefore prefer each other to the smaller country, while the smaller country is indifferent between them. This is a partner-ranking result, not a coalition-stability theorem, and Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability} does not require market-size asymmetry. More broadly, the model's industrial-organization contribution is the feedback from firm conduct to institutional incentives: standards shape product-market competition, private compatibility choices alter profits and national welfare, and those continuation payoffs determine blocking incentives. The standards-economics implication is correspondingly that compatibility affects not only market access and competition but also the political value of formal standard-setting arrangements. From a coalition-formation perspective, formal membership alone need not fix coalition payoffs when post-formation private responses are endogenous.

The institutional implication is deliberately narrower than a policy prescription. Private compatibility and public harmonization need not be political substitutes even when compatibility reduces the technological consequences of incompatible standards. Changes in the cost of redesign, multi-standard production, certification, or other compatibility investments can redistribute rents across formal arrangements and may therefore alter government support for regional versus broader harmonization. The paper characterizes coalition stability, however, not global social optimality. The finding that international standardization becomes uniquely stable in the intermediate region should not be read as showing that it maximizes world welfare, nor does instability imply that a regional arrangement is inefficient. The mechanism instead suggests an empirical question for future work: whether declines in private interoperability costs are associated with changes in political support for exclusive regional versus broader standards arrangements.

The formal results are derived in a deliberately parsimonious three-country Cournot environment with one firm per country, binary compatibility adoption, an exogenous fixed adoption cost, and strict blocking in an exclusive-membership partition game without transfers. Larger coalition networks, bargaining and side payments, alternative forms of oligopoly competition, and endogenous compatibility investment may change adoption incentives or coalition deviations and therefore require separate analysis. Two natural next steps are to examine whether selective erosion survives in richer coalition networks and to endogenize firms' compatibility investment while preserving the distinction between technological adaptation and formal membership. Within the present model, the central lesson is sharper: private compatibility matters not only because it changes how firms compete across standards, but also because it changes the continuation payoffs that determine which formal standards arrangements governments are willing to sustain.
