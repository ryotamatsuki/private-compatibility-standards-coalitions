\section{Private Compatibility Decisions}
\label{sec:private-compatibility}

This section solves the private-adoption stage using the Cournot profit blocks from Section~\ref{sec:product-market}. The formal partition is held fixed. A firm may add support for one of the other formal standards by paying the fixed cost $F$ once for that standard; the same adoption can then be used in every market covered by the adopted standard. This fixed technological investment is distinct from the marginal adaptation cost $c$, which is incurred on units sold in a market whose local standard the firm does not support. Because product markets are segmented and operating profits add across destinations, private-adoption incentives can be written as differences between the verified profit blocks. Formal coalition membership is never changed by these decisions. This representation also separates the discrete investment problem from the Cournot stage. Conditional on an adoption profile, Section~\ref{sec:product-market} already supplies the relevant operating profit in every destination; a firm therefore compares the sum of the affected post-adoption blocks with the corresponding pre-adoption blocks and subtracts one fixed cost for each newly supported standard. No product-market quantity choice is reopened at the adoption stage. This block-difference method makes the geographic scope of a standard explicit and prevents a market-level profit gain from being confused with a standard-level fixed cost.

Under international standardization, $\rho^{IS}$ contains only one formal standard and every firm supports it natively. There is therefore no additional-standard adoption decision under IS. This absence of adoption is technological, not a consequence of a high value of $F$.

\subsection{Adoption gains and threshold notation}
\label{subsec:private-thresholds}

Three market-level profit differences summarize the relevant adoption incentives:
\begin{equation}
    T_A\equiv P-B,
    \qquad
    T_U\equiv A-C,
    \qquad
    T_W\equiv A-S.
    \label{eq:private-threshold-definitions}
\end{equation}
Each difference has a specific before--after interpretation. $T_A$ is the gain of an excluded costly singleton that adopts the local standard after the other two firms are already compatible: its operating-profit block changes from $B$ to $P$. $T_U$ is the gain of a regional-union member that unilaterally adopts the outsider's standard in the outsider market: its block changes from $C$ to $A$. $T_W$ is the gain of a foreign firm under separate standards that unilaterally adopts the target country's standard: its block changes from $S$ to $A$. Thus $T_A$, $T_U$, and $T_W$ are endogenous profit differences generated by the product-market equilibrium, not exogenous switching-cost parameters. They are market-level sufficient statistics: once the relevant pre-adoption state is known, the variable-profit return from adoption is summarized by one of these three objects. The distinction between them matters because a firm that adopts first generally starts from a different Cournot configuration than a firm that adopts after a rival. In particular, $T_U$ and $T_W$ describe unilateral moves from two different baseline regimes, whereas $T_A$ describes the common late-adoption move from exclusion to complete compatibility.

The canonical expressions imply
\begin{equation}
    T_U
    =\frac{3c(2-c)(1-v)}{4(2-3v)^2},
    \label{eq:TU-closed-form}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    T_W-T_U
    =\frac{v(1-2c)^2(8-9v)}{16(2-3v)^2}>0.
    \label{eq:TW-minus-TU}
\end{equation}
Hence $T_W>T_U>0$ on the canonical domain. Positivity of $T_A$ also follows analytically from that domain. The excluded quantity $q_B$ is positive and satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial q_B}{\partial c}
    =-\frac{3(1-v)}{2(2-3v)}<0,
    \label{eq:qB-c-derivative}
\end{equation}
so $B=q_B^2$ decreases with $c$. At $c=0$,
\begin{equation}
    \left.(P-B)\right|_{c=0}
    =\frac{v(36v^2-51v+16)}{16(1-v)(2-3v)^2}>0.
    \label{eq:TA-at-zero-c}
\end{equation}
The quadratic in the numerator is decreasing on $0<v<1/4$ and equals $11/2$ at $v=1/4$. Therefore $T_A>0$ for every admissible $c>0$. We do not impose any additional ordering between $T_A$ and either $T_U$ or $T_W$.

\subsection{Regional standardization}
\label{subsec:private-su}

Consider a regional coalition $C=\{i,j\}$ with outsider $o$. The outsider can adopt the bloc standard. In each coalition-member market, its pre-adoption operating-profit block is $B$ and its post-adoption block is $P$. Because one adoption of the bloc standard covers both member markets, the outsider pays $F$ once while collecting the operating-profit gain in both destinations. With general market masses,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_o^{SU}
    =(m_i+m_j)(P-B)-F
    =(m_i+m_j)T_A-F.
    \label{eq:su-outsider-adoption-gain}
\end{equation}
In the symmetric Main Model this becomes
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_o^{SU}=2T_A-F,
    \qquad
    a_o=1\iff F<2T_A.
    \label{eq:su-outsider-main-threshold}
\end{equation}
At $F=2T_A$, the outsider is indifferent. The factor two in \eqref{eq:su-outsider-main-threshold} is therefore a geographic-scope effect of the bloc standard, not a duplication of the fixed adoption cost.

The two coalition members face a separate binary adoption subgame over the outsider's standard. Suppose first that member $j$ has not adopted. If member $i$ adopts unilaterally, its operating-profit block in the outsider market changes from $C$ to $A$. Thus
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_i^{SU}(a_j=0)
    =m_o(A-C)-F
    =m_oT_U-F.
    \label{eq:su-member-unilateral-gain}
\end{equation}
In the Main Model, $i$ adopts when $F<T_U$. If instead member $j$ has already adopted the outsider's standard, member $i$ becomes the excluded costly singleton in that market. Its own adoption then changes its profit from $B$ to $P$, so
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_i^{SU}(a_j=1)
    =m_o(P-B)-F
    =m_oT_A-F.
    \label{eq:su-member-post-rival-gain}
\end{equation}
The corresponding Main-Model threshold is $F<T_A$. The rival's prior adoption therefore changes the relevant pre-adoption product-market position and, with it, the profit difference governing the best response. The outsider's bloc-standard decision and the members' outsider-standard subgame are otherwise separable. The former changes the outsider's position in the two member markets, while the latter changes the members' positions only in the outsider market. Each decision also concerns a different additional formal standard and therefore carries its own fixed cost. This is why the outsider's threshold aggregates two copies of $T_A$, whereas a member's threshold contains only one outsider-market gain. We do not classify the member subgame globally as exhibiting strategic complementarity or substitutability, because doing so would require an ordering between $T_A$ and $T_U$ that is not imposed here. The full equilibrium correspondence outside the regions used below is left to \ref{app:adoption-equilibria}.

\subsection{Separate national standards}
\label{subsec:private-sw}

Under $\rho^{SW}$, each country has its own formal standard. Fix a target country $k$ and let $i,j\neq k$ denote the two foreign firms. If neither foreign firm has adopted country $k$'s standard, unilateral adoption by firm $i$ changes its target-market profit from $S$ to $A$. Hence
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_i^{SW}(a_j^k=0)
    =m_k(A-S)-F
    =m_kT_W-F.
    \label{eq:sw-unilateral-gain}
\end{equation}
In the Main Model the unilateral threshold is $F<T_W$. If foreign firm $j$ has already adopted the target standard, the remaining foreign firm is the excluded costly singleton. Adoption then changes its profit from $B$ to $P$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta_i^{SW}(a_j^k=1)
    =m_k(P-B)-F
    =m_kT_A-F.
    \label{eq:sw-post-rival-gain}
\end{equation}
The Main-Model post-rival threshold is therefore $F<T_A$.

This target-standard decomposition is exact because markets are segmented and the fixed cost is additive across additional standards. Under SW, adopting country $k$'s standard affects the product-market configuration in market $k$ and costs $F$ for that standard; adoption decisions concerning another country's standard constitute a separate target-standard subgame. A firm can in principle support both foreign standards, but those two investments simply add two standard-specific fixed costs and the corresponding destination-specific operating-profit changes. Hence solving the binary subgame for each target standard is sufficient to reconstruct the firm's worldwide private-adoption payoff. The same $T_A$ appears in both SU and SW because, after one rival has adopted, the remaining non-adopter starts from the same excluded-singleton profit block $B$ and reaches the same complete-compatibility block $P$. This repeated threshold is therefore a consequence of a repeated product-market state, not an additional assumption about firms' adoption technology.

\subsection{Relevant fixed-cost regions}
\label{subsec:private-regions}

For the subsequent analysis it is convenient to define
\begin{equation}
    F_L\equiv\max\{T_W,T_A\},
    \qquad
    F^\ast\equiv2T_A.
    \label{eq:private-F-boundaries}
\end{equation}
The lower boundary $F_L$ is large enough to shut down both unilateral SW adoption, governed by $T_W$, and any post-rival adoption, governed by $T_A$. The upper boundary $F^\ast$ is the threshold for the SU outsider's adoption of the two-country bloc standard. The canonical parameter domain alone does not imply $F_L<F^\ast$; the following characterization is conditional on that additional ordering.

If
\begin{equation}
    F_L<F<F^\ast,
    \label{eq:intermediate-F-region}
\end{equation}
then the SU outsider adopts because $F<2T_A$. Coalition members do not adopt the outsider standard: $F>F_L\ge T_A$ rules out adoption after the other member has adopted, while $F>F_L\ge T_W>T_U$ rules out unilateral adoption. Thus the unique relevant private-adoption pattern under each SU is outsider-only adoption. This statement is selection free: each member strictly prefers non-adoption regardless of the other member's action, while the outsider strictly prefers adoption. Under SW, $F>T_W$ eliminates unilateral target-standard adoption and $F>T_A$ eliminates post-rival adoption. Non-adoption is therefore a strict best response for each foreign firm whether or not its rival adopts, so every target-standard subgame has the unique no-adoption outcome. IS has no additional-standard decision. If instead $F>F^\ast$ under the same ordering $F_L<F^\ast$, then $F>2T_A$, $F>T_A$, and $F>T_W$; consequently no private adoption occurs under SU or SW either. The role of the two boundaries is thus purely technological at this stage: they separate private continuation patterns before any government comparison is made.

\begin{lemma}[Private-adoption incentives and relevant thresholds]
\label{lem:private-adoption}
On the canonical parameter domain, $T_A,T_U,T_W>0$ and $T_W>T_U$. In the symmetric Main Model, the SU outsider adopts the bloc standard if and only if $F<2T_A$. An SU member adopts the outsider standard if $F<T_U$ when the other member has not adopted and if $F<T_A$ when the other member has adopted. Under SW, a foreign firm adopts a target country's standard if $F<T_W$ when the rival foreign firm has not adopted and if $F<T_A$ when the rival has adopted. Whenever $F_L<F^\ast$, every $F\in(F_L,F^\ast)$ yields outsider-only adoption under each SU and no private adoption under SW or IS; for $F>F^\ast$ under the same ordering, no private adoption occurs under SU or SW.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:TU-closed-form} is strictly positive because the canonical domain implies $0<c<1/3<2$ and $0<v<1$. Equation~\eqref{eq:TW-minus-TU} is strictly positive because $v>0$, $c<1/3$ implies $1-2c>0$, and $8-9v>0$ for $v<1/4$; hence $T_W>T_U>0$. For $T_A$, \eqref{eq:qB-c-derivative} and positivity of $q_B$ imply that $B=q_B^2$ falls with $c$. Equation~\eqref{eq:TA-at-zero-c} is positive because $36v^2-51v+16$ is decreasing over the admissible interval and remains positive at its upper endpoint. Thus $T_A>0$. Equations~\eqref{eq:su-outsider-adoption-gain}--\eqref{eq:sw-post-rival-gain} then give the four relevant before--after operating-profit differences after subtracting the fixed cost. Imposing $m_1=m_2=m_3=1$ gives the stated best-response thresholds. Finally, \eqref{eq:private-F-boundaries} and $T_W>T_U$ imply all of the strict inequalities used in \eqref{eq:intermediate-F-region}; the high-$F$ claim follows from $F>2T_A>T_A$ together with $2T_A=F^\ast>F_L\ge T_W$.
\end{proof}

At a threshold equality---$F=2T_A$, $F=T_U$, $F=T_A$, or $F=T_W$---the relevant firm is indifferent. Consistent with the timing in Section~\ref{sec:model}, the private-adoption equilibrium is then understood as a correspondence rather than imposing an arbitrary selection rule. Private adoption therefore trades a standard-specific fixed technological cost against operating-profit gains generated by compatibility and the removal of marginal incompatibility costs. Its return can span several destinations when a single formal standard covers a bloc, and a firm's incentive can change after a rival adopts because the firm's pre-adoption Cournot position changes. None of these technological choices changes the formal partition $\rho$. These private-adoption outcomes determine the product-market configuration that governments anticipate when comparing formal standards regimes. Section~\ref{sec:selective-erosion} studies how the relevant private response changes those continuation comparisons.
