\section{Secondary Results}
\label{sec:secondary}

The symmetry used for the Main theorem is deliberate: it shows that market-size heterogeneity is not needed for the stability reversal in Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability}. We now introduce a one-dimensional market-size asymmetry for a separate purpose---to determine partner rankings among regional standards unions. This extension therefore sharpens the identity of a preferred regional partner without changing the selective-erosion mechanism or the parameter requirements of the Main stability result.

\subsection{Market-size asymmetry and partner ranking}
\label{subsec:market-size-partner-ranking}

Consider the secondary specification
\begin{equation}
    m_1=m_2=1,
    \qquad
    m_3=1-\delta,
    \qquad
    0<\delta<1.
    \label{eq:secondary-market-sizes}
\end{equation}
Countries 1 and 2 therefore have equal larger markets, while country 3 has the smaller market. No other primitive is asymmetric. In particular, costs, preferences, network effects, and government objectives remain unchanged. We condition on a continuation state with no private standard adoption so that the result isolates partner selection from the selective-erosion mechanism. A sufficiently high fixed cost provides one such continuation state, but the payoff identity below requires only that no private adoption occurs.

This conditioning separates two distinct comparative questions. Sections~\ref{sec:private-compatibility}--\ref{sec:coalition-stability} ask whether firms circumvent a given formal partition and how that response changes governments' incentives over formal regimes. The present section instead holds the private-adoption state fixed and asks how a government ranks two regional coalitions that differ only in the market size of its partner. Consequently, $F$ does not enter the partner-ranking difference directly. Its role here is only to support the no-adoption continuation state under which the comparison is evaluated. This separation is useful because it makes clear that market-size asymmetry is not doing hidden work in the Main stability reversal.

Let country $i$ compare two otherwise identical regional standards unions, one with partner $j$ and outsider $k$, and the other with partner $k$ and outsider $j$. From the general-mass continuation payoff in Section~\ref{sec:selective-erosion}, country $i$ obtains
\begin{align}
    W_i^{ij}
    &=m_iK_M+(m_i+m_j)A+m_kC,
    \label{eq:partner-payoff-ij}\\
    W_i^{ik}
    &=m_iK_M+(m_i+m_k)A+m_jC.
    \label{eq:partner-payoff-ik}
\end{align}
Subtracting gives the general partner-comparison identity
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    W_i^{ij}-W_i^{ik}
    =(m_j-m_k)(A-C)
    =(m_j-m_k)T_U.
    }
    \label{eq:general-partner-ranking}
\end{equation}
The consumer-surplus term in country $i$'s own market cancels. Replacing a smaller partner with a larger partner instead reallocates market mass from the outsider-profit block $C$ to the coalition-member profit block $A$.

The accounting in \eqref{eq:general-partner-ranking} also clarifies why the result is a partner-size effect rather than a generic preference for larger markets. Country $i$'s own market mass and its own-market consumer surplus are identical across the two coalitions. The only change is where the other countries' market mass appears in the domestic firm's worldwide operating profit. A unit of market mass attached to a coalition partner contributes the member-market block $A$; the same unit of mass attached to the outsider contributes the outsider-market block $C$. Partner selection is therefore governed by the difference between these two profit opportunities, not by a direct utility benefit from country size itself.

The sign is immediate from the private-adoption block already derived in Section~\ref{sec:private-compatibility}:
\begin{equation}
    A-C=T_U
    =\frac{3c(2-c)(1-v)}{4(2-3v)^2}>0
    \label{eq:partner-ranking-tu}
\end{equation}
throughout the canonical admissible domain. The same primitive profit difference thus appears on a different strategic margin. In Section~\ref{sec:private-compatibility}, $A-C$ determines the unilateral incentive to adopt the outsider standard. Here it measures the marginal value of moving market mass from outsider treatment to coalition-member treatment. This connection does not make market-size asymmetry part of the selective-erosion mechanism.

\subsection{Market-Size Partner Selection}
\label{subsec:market-size-partner-selection}

Under \eqref{eq:secondary-market-sizes}, country 1's welfare in the two relevant regional coalitions is
\begin{align}
    W_1^{12}
    &=K_M+2A+(1-\delta)C,
    \label{eq:w1-su12}\\
    W_1^{13}
    &=K_M+(2-\delta)A+C.
    \label{eq:w1-su13}
\end{align}
Hence
\begin{equation}
    W_1^{12}-W_1^{13}
    =\delta(A-C)
    =\delta T_U>0.
    \label{eq:w1-partner-ranking}
\end{equation}
The analogous comparison for country 2 is identical. Country 3, by contrast, faces two potential partners of equal market mass and is therefore indifferent between them.

\begin{proposition}[Market-Size Partner Selection]
\label{prop:market-size-partner-selection}
Suppose $m_1=m_2=1$, $m_3=1-\delta$, with $0<\delta<1$, and condition on a continuation state with no private standard adoption. Then
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    W_1^{12}-W_1^{13}
    =W_2^{12}-W_2^{23}
    =\delta(A-C)
    =\delta T_U>0,
    }
    \label{eq:large-country-partner-ranking}
\end{equation}
while
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    W_3^{13}=W_3^{23}.
    }
    \label{eq:small-country-partner-indifference}
\end{equation}
Thus each larger country strictly prefers the other larger country to the smaller country as its regional-standard partner, whereas the smaller country is indifferent between the two larger partners.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:general-partner-ranking} gives
\[
    W_i^{ij}-W_i^{ik}=(m_j-m_k)(A-C).
\]
For country 1, $m_2-m_3=\delta$, and for country 2, $m_1-m_3=\delta$. Since $A-C=T_U>0$ and $\delta>0$, both differences are strictly positive. For country 3, the two potential partners have equal masses, $m_1=m_2$, so the corresponding difference is zero.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:market-size-partner-selection} is a partner-preference result, not a coalition-stability theorem. For example, $W_1^{12}>W_1^{13}$ by itself does not imply that $\rho_{13}^{SU}$ is strictly blocked by $\{1,2\}$: strict blocking would additionally require country 2 to gain from the same induced deviation relative to its own current payoff. The proposition therefore does not establish a uniquely stable $\rho_{12}^{SU}$ and does not modify the stable-set result in Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability}.

This distinction matters because the asymmetry removes one source of indifference without, by itself, solving the full coalition-formation problem. In the symmetric high-$F$ analysis, an alternative regional pair could not strictly block an existing SU because the country common to the two coalitions received the same member payoff. Proposition~\ref{prop:market-size-partner-selection} shows that this particular indifference can disappear for a larger country when the prospective partners have different market sizes. Whether an actual partner-switching deviation is a strict block, however, also depends on the payoff of the second deviator. Establishing that additional inequality would be a separate result, not a corollary of Proposition~\ref{prop:market-size-partner-selection}; we therefore do not impose an unverified partner-switching region here.

Two comparative observations make the role of the asymmetry transparent. First, letting $\delta\downarrow0$ restores the symmetric Main Model and
\begin{equation}
    \left.(W_1^{12}-W_1^{13})\right|_{\delta=0}=0,
    \label{eq:delta-symmetry-restoration}
\end{equation}
which is consistent with the inability of the symmetric high-$F$ model to select a unique regional partner. Second,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial\delta}
    \left(W_1^{12}-W_1^{13}\right)
    =T_U>0.
    \label{eq:delta-partner-comparative-static}
\end{equation}
A larger market-size gap therefore strengthens the larger country's ranking in favor of the other larger partner. This derivative concerns only the payoff difference across potential partners; it is not a welfare statement about shrinking country 3. Nor does it imply that a larger $\delta$ monotonically changes the stable partition set, because such a claim would require the complete set of strict-blocking inequalities under asymmetric market sizes.

A larger partner is valuable not because market-size asymmetry creates selective erosion, but because it reallocates market mass from the outsider-profit block $C$ to the member-market block $A$. The extension consequently sharpens partner identity while leaving the Main theoretical chain intact: symmetry establishes the private-compatibility stability mechanism, whereas asymmetry only refines which regional partner a government prefers.
