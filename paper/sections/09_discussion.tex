\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The analysis separates three objects that can easily be conflated: technological compatibility, government incentives, and formal coalition stability. A private workaround directly changes only the first. Its effect on the second depends on which rents survive the workaround, and its effect on the third additionally depends on the coalition institution.

\subsection{Economic interpretation}
\label{subsec:discussion-interpretation}

The member government's initial regional advantage is $\mathscr E-\mathscr D$; after outsider adoption, it is $-\mathscr D+R(d,v)$. The rent $R(d,v)$ arises from product-market equilibrium, not an assumed erosion coefficient. Adopting the regional standard changes consumer surplus and member firms' operating profits in opposite directions. In the reversal region, the consumers' gains do not offset the losses of protected domestic producers, while the member's disadvantage in the outsider market remains. Where higher residual adaptation costs limit competitive erosion, this national-welfare comparison can move in the opposite direction. Because the government values its own consumers and domestic producer's worldwide profits, but not the foreign adopter's profits, technical convergence need not be politically equivalent to formal harmonization.

Low residual costs can therefore strengthen or reverse the government's preference for international standardization; intermediate costs may strengthen that incentive without changing the ranking; and larger residual costs may weaken it. This conditionality follows from the interplay of consumer and producer rents, not from compatibility as an unconditional political substitute or complement.

The fixed adoption cost $F$ selects the private continuation, whereas $d$ governs the rent remaining after adoption. A government-ranking comparison at a private action that firms would not choose is economically uninformative. The joint open-set result therefore verifies firm incentives and government preferences using identical primitives; the sign partition alone is not the contribution.

\subsection{What the robustness results imply}
\label{subsec:discussion-robustness}

The negative portability results discipline the interpretation. At $v=0$, private one-way adoption can still occur while the initial SU advantage disappears. Under the pre-specified singleton-network alternative, the same separation arises. Under the differentiated-demand test, outsider-only adoption again survives while the member-market political term changes sign in both Cournot and Bertrand versions.

The firm-side asymmetric-adoption mechanism survives more specifications than the government-ranking result. To sustain the latter, regional exclusion must initially generate a sufficiently positive member-market surplus. The canonical Cournot model supplies this under specified conditions; the differentiated-demand comparison does not. Moreover, assigning no network benefit to a singleton is substantive: it describes spillovers among mutually compatible producers, whereas a standalone network with independent value would change regional rents. The failed specifications show that private adaptation is not sufficient for a public harmonization incentive.

The coalition analysis adds another layer. The intermediate unique-IS application is based on strict payoff differences and survives both strict and weak blocking under the certified asymmetry perturbation. By contrast, the high-$F$ multiplicity of symmetric regional unions relies on an indifference that disappears under weak blocking or small market-size asymmetry. The economically robust statement is therefore about continuation-payoff changes, not about one particular stable-set correspondence.

\subsection{Institutional interpretation and empirical use}
\label{subsec:discussion-institutions}

The formal partitions are stylized representations of fragmented, regional, and common standards regimes. Private adoption can be read as redesign, multi-standard production, dual certification, interface adaptation, or another costly action that allows a firm to support an additional formal standard. The model does not identify which real-world technology corresponds exactly to $F$ or $d$. It isolates the economic distinction between a fixed investment needed to support another standard and the residual per-unit burden that may remain after doing so.

Potentially observable counterparts are the one-time cost $F$ of supporting an additional standard, the residual per-unit adaptation cost $d$, and whether outsider compliance creates reciprocal access for coalition members. Relating the model to any real agreement would also require evidence on protected consumer and producer rents, and governments' available alternatives. This paper provides neither an empirical identification strategy nor an implementable harmonization instrument.

The coalition result should likewise not be read as a welfare theorem. In the certified intermediate region, IS Pareto-dominates the other four formal partitions within the model's five-partition menu. No unrestricted planner problem is solved, and nothing here establishes that international standardization is globally first best.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

The model has three countries, one firm per country, segmented linear demand, binary adoption, exogenous fixed costs and a restricted partition menu. Compatibility remains binary when cost relief is incomplete. The positive-residual-cost theorem concerns government \emph{preferences}, not coalition stability at positive $d$; the latter application uses full bypass. Entry, heterogeneous firms, continuous compatibility and renegotiation could change these outcomes.

The alternative demand and singleton-network specifications document specific failures; they do not establish that no differentiated-product, Bertrand or alternative-network setting can support the mechanism. The positive result is therefore model-specific, not functionally general.

Finally, coalition stability is static. The analysis does not model accession negotiations, farsighted deviations, transfers, repeated interaction, or sunk adoption investments carried across institutional transitions. Those extensions could change the mapping from continuation payoffs to stable formal arrangements even when the product-market mechanism remains the same.
