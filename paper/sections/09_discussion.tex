\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The formal results in Sections~\ref{sec:selective-erosion}--\ref{sec:secondary} identify a simple mechanism with an institutional implication that is less immediate than the underlying technology. Private compatibility matters politically because it changes the continuation value of formal exclusion. A firm can support an additional standard without changing the formal partition, yet that private technological response alters the product-market rents attached to the partition and therefore changes governments' incentives over formal standards regimes. The paper's central contribution is consequently not that private compatibility makes incompatible standards less costly. It is that private adaptation can change the relative political value of alternative formal standards institutions.

\subsection{From technological adaptation to political incentives}
\label{subsec:discussion-mechanism}

The mechanism is clearest in the continuation-payoff decomposition for a member of a regional standards union. Without private bypass,
\begin{equation}
    W_i^{SU,N}-W_i^{IS}
    =\mathscr E_i-\mathscr D_i,
    \label{eq:discussion-before}
\end{equation}
where $\mathscr E_i=m_i(K_M-K_I)+M_C(A-P)$ is the member government's net exclusion surplus generated in coalition-member markets and $\mathscr D_i=m_o(P-C)$ is its reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market. The first object is a national-welfare term, not a pure producer rent: it combines the change in domestic consumer surplus with the domestic firm's operating-profit difference across coalition-member destinations. The second captures the profit disadvantage that the same domestic firm bears when serving the outsider market under a standard that remains incompatible there.

Outsider-only private compatibility affects these components asymmetrically. By adopting the bloc standard, the outsider becomes compatible in the coalition-member markets. The member's consumer-surplus and domestic-firm profit blocks in those markets move to their complete-compatibility counterparts, so the coalition-specific component $\mathscr E_i$ disappears. Coalition members, however, have not adopted the outsider's standard. Their firms therefore remain disadvantaged in the outsider market, and $\mathscr D_i$ survives. The post-bypass comparison is
\begin{equation}
    W_i^{SU,O}-W_i^{IS}
    =-\mathscr D_i.
    \label{eq:discussion-after}
\end{equation}
The central transformation is thus $\mathscr E_i-\mathscr D_i\rightarrow-\mathscr D_i$.

This is why the erosion is selective rather than complete. Private compatibility does not turn the regional standards union into international standardization, and it does not admit the outsider to the formal coalition. The partition remains $\rho_{ij}^{SU}$. What changes is the continuation payoff generated by that partition. The outsider's technological response removes the part of the regional arrangement that benefited members through exclusion in coalition markets while leaving the members' reciprocal disadvantage outside the bloc intact. In this sense, private technological adaptation changes the political value of a formal institutional boundary without directly changing the boundary itself.

The same accounting explains the ``technological substitute, political complement'' interpretation in Corollary~\ref{cor:technological-political}. Private compatibility is a technological substitute for multilateral harmonization in the narrow sense that it reduces the effective compatibility difference between SU and IS in coalition-member markets. It need not be a political substitute. From Section~\ref{sec:selective-erosion},
\[
    G_i^O-G_i^N=\mathscr E_i.
\]
When $\mathscr E_i>0$, outsider-only compatibility raises the member government's relative gain from moving to international standardization by exactly the rent that private bypass removes. By eroding a payoff component specific to the discriminatory regional arrangement, private compatibility may therefore make broader harmonization politically more attractive. This is a binary government-incentive comparison in the identified model region, not a claim of general supermodularity or continuous-action complementarity.

This feedback is also the paper's industrial-organization channel. Formal standards first shape compatibility relations and marginal costs in product markets. Firms then choose whether the operating-profit gains from supporting an additional standard justify the fixed cost $F$. Those choices change Cournot competition, profits, and consumer surplus under each formal partition. Governments evaluate the resulting continuation outcomes rather than the formal partition in isolation. The causal chain is therefore firm conduct $\rightarrow$ product-market outcomes $\rightarrow$ coalition-specific rents $\rightarrow$ government incentives over formal standards regimes. Private compatibility is not an exogenous reduction in all bilateral frictions: adoption is strategic, standard-specific, costly, and dependent on the current formal partition.

Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability} shows how this firm-level response reaches formal coalition stability. At high $F$, private circumvention is inactive. Regional members retain the exclusion advantage that makes $W_M^{SU,N}>W^{IS}$ on $\Omega_0$, and all three symmetric regional standards unions are stable. When $F$ falls into the intermediate region $F_L<F<2T_A$, the outsider alone finds bypass profitable. The member payoff becomes $W_M^{SU,O}<W^{IS}$, while the outsider also strictly prefers IS. The grand coalition then strictly blocks every regional SU, and international standardization is uniquely stable under the maintained strict-blocking concept. Thus lower private compatibility costs can destabilize regional formal cooperation over the identified range by changing continuation payoffs rather than by directly changing coalition membership.

The intermediate region is economically useful because the result is not attached to a threshold equality. Section~\ref{sec:coalition-stability} establishes an open set of product-market parameters for which $F_L<2T_A$, leaving a nondegenerate interval of compatibility costs that selects outsider-only bypass. The paper does not infer monotonicity outside that interval. When compatibility becomes sufficiently cheap for broader multistandarding patterns to arise, additional adoption outcomes appear; those low-$F$ cases are outside the headline stability comparison and are treated separately.

\subsection{Institutional interpretation}
\label{subsec:discussion-institutions}

The formal partitions are best read as stylized institutional arrangements. Separate national standards represent fragmentation, a regional standards union represents an exclusive harmonization arrangement among a subset of countries, and international standardization represents a common standard covering all three. These categories capture the economic distinction between fragmented, exclusive, and universal compatibility regimes; they are not intended as literal descriptions of any single standards organization or agreement.

The private compatibility technology should be interpreted with similar discipline. Paying $F$ is a reduced-form representation of costly actions that allow a firm to support an additional formal standard. Depending on the application, such actions might resemble redesign, multi-standard production, dual certification, interface adaptation, compliance engineering, or another compatibility layer. The model does not distinguish among these technologies. What matters for the mechanism is that the investment is privately chosen, discrete, and tied to a particular additional standard, so its return depends on the set of markets covered by that standard.

The fixed cost $F$ therefore has an institutional interpretation beyond being a technical bifurcation parameter. When $F$ is high, formal standards boundaries have substantial economic bite because firms do not find it profitable to circumvent them. In the intermediate region, the outsider can profitably support the two-country bloc standard while coalition members still decline to support the outsider's standard. This directional response is precisely what creates selective erosion. The result should not be read as saying that lower compatibility costs mechanically favor international standardization at every cost level. Rather, the relative political value of formal harmonization depends on which firms find circumvention profitable and which coalition-specific rents their investments remove.

This distinction also disciplines the policy interpretation. Formal harmonization and private compatibility need not be substitutes from the government's perspective even when they are technological substitutes in product markets. A change in the cost or availability of interoperability can redistribute rents across formal arrangements and thereby alter political support for regional versus broader harmonization. The model, however, does not provide a social-planner ranking of those arrangements. Stability should not be conflated with efficiency: $\rho^{IS}$ being uniquely stable in the intermediate region does not establish that international standardization maximizes world welfare, nor does instability of a regional SU establish that regional harmonization is inefficient. Government preference in the model is a national-welfare comparison used to evaluate strict blocking.

The mechanism suggests empirical questions without itself providing empirical validation. One could ask whether declines in the cost of multi-standard production, interoperability investment, or cross-standard certification are associated with weaker dependence on regional exclusivity and changes in government support for broader harmonization. Such patterns would require institution-specific measurement and a separate identification strategy. The current model supplies a mechanism linking these objects; it does not establish their empirical prevalence or causal magnitude.

\subsection{Scope and robustness of the mechanism}
\label{subsec:discussion-scope}

The symmetric Main Model is useful precisely because it separates the stability mechanism from partner selection. Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability} requires no market-size asymmetry: $m_1=m_2=m_3=1$ is sufficient for the private-compatibility-induced transition from the set of regional SUs to IS. Section~\ref{sec:secondary} introduces $m_3=1-\delta$ only to sharpen which regional partner a government prefers in a no-adoption continuation state. Proposition~\ref{prop:market-size-partner-selection} therefore does not establish coalition stability, and market-size heterogeneity does not generate the selective-erosion mechanism.

Within the model, the logic points to four ingredients. First, an exclusive regional arrangement creates a member-specific continuation advantage in some markets. Second, a private technological response can remove that advantage. Third, some disadvantage associated with remaining outside the broader arrangement survives the response. Fourth, governments compare formal institutions using the continuation payoffs generated after private adaptation. The Cournot microfoundation makes these components explicit as $\mathscr E_i$ and $\mathscr D_i$. This accounting suggests a mechanism based on selective rather than uniform erosion of institutional rents, but the paper does not prove that the same ranking reversal holds under arbitrary oligopoly structures.

Several features are correspondingly not part of the substantive claim. Market-size asymmetry is not essential, and the symmetric high-$F$ result does not require selection of a unique regional partner. Network effects enter the canonical demand system and affect the quantitative payoff blocks, but the selective-erosion mechanism should not be interpreted as requiring network effects per se. Conversely, the analysis does not show that network effects are irrelevant; they remain part of the product-market environment in which the verified open parameter region is constructed.

Other features are model-specific and delimit the theorem's reach. There are three countries and one firm per country, firms compete in quantities, demand is linear, compatibility adoption is binary, the formal coalition menu is exogenous, and governments maximize national welfare. The stability result uses strict blocking in an exclusive-membership partition game. Alternative notions such as consent-based accession, farsighted stability, coalition-proofness, or bargaining-based formation are not analyzed. These restrictions are useful because they make it possible to solve the private-adoption stage and all relevant blocking comparisons exactly; they should not be mistaken for verified robustness results.

The three-country environment is intentionally minimal. It is rich enough to distinguish fragmentation, exclusive regional harmonization, and universal harmonization while keeping private adoption and coalition blocking tractable. With more countries, one would need to address coalition size, multiple competing blocs, nested or overlapping compatibility structures, and potentially path-dependent accession. Those possibilities may alter both private adoption incentives and the set of admissible coalition deviations. Extending the mechanism to larger coalition networks therefore requires additional coalition-formation structure rather than a mechanical replication of the three-country proof.

Cournot competition serves a related purpose. It yields closed-form quantities and profit blocks that connect compatibility decisions directly to government continuation payoffs. The current theorem does not establish the same stability reversal under differentiated Bertrand competition, heterogeneous firms, or endogenous entry. Those environments are natural tests of whether the selective-erosion accounting survives different forms of strategic competition, but they are outside the frozen model.

\subsection{Limitations and directions for further work}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

Two further restrictions are especially relevant for institutional applications. First, compatibility is binary and its fixed cost is exogenous. In practice, interoperability can be partial, quality dependent, or built through cumulative investment, and its cost may depend on R\&D, accumulated technical knowledge, certification infrastructure, or regulatory design. Endogenizing the compatibility technology could create feedback from expected institutional outcomes to firms' investment incentives. That possibility is distinct from the present result, which takes $F$ as a primitive and endogenizes only the adoption decision.

Second, the model excludes transfers, side payments, and coalition bargaining. Strict blocking therefore depends on each deviator's own national-welfare gain. Allowing transfers could decouple aggregate coalition gains from individual blocking incentives and is a substantive extension rather than a normalization. Relatedly, the model does not study accession negotiations, endogenous standards design, repeated interaction, dynamic investment, or incomplete information. Each could change the distribution or persistence of coalition-specific rents.

These limitations qualify the scope of the formal theorem but leave the mechanism established within the model intact. Private adaptation changes the continuation value of formal exclusion; when it selectively erodes the member-market advantage that supports a regional coalition while leaving reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market, it can reverse government rankings and alter the strict-blocking stable set. The next section concludes by summarizing that mechanism and its implication for the relationship between private compatibility and formal standards cooperation.
