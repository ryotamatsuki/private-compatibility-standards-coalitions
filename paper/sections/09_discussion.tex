\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The formal results in Sections~\ref{sec:selective-erosion}--\ref{sec:coalition-stability} show why private compatibility can matter politically even when it leaves formal coalition membership unchanged. Outsider-only bypass removes the member-market exclusion component that supports a regional standards union while leaving coalition members' reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market intact. The resulting selective erosion can reverse members' ranking between regional and international standardization and, when the outsider also prefers the international regime, change the strict-blocking stable set. The substantive point is therefore not that private compatibility simply reduces the technological cost of incompatibility. It is that a private technological response can change the continuation value of a formal institutional boundary.

\subsection{Institutional interpretation}
\label{subsec:discussion-institutions}

The formal partitions are best read as stylized institutional arrangements. Separate national standards represent fragmentation, a regional standards union represents an exclusive harmonization arrangement among a subset of countries, and international standardization represents a common standard covering all three. These categories capture the economic distinction between fragmented, exclusive, and universal compatibility regimes; they are not intended as literal descriptions of any single standards organization or agreement.

The private compatibility technology should be interpreted with similar discipline. Paying $F$ is a reduced-form representation of costly actions that allow a firm to support an additional formal standard. Depending on the application, such actions might resemble redesign, multi-standard production, dual certification, interface adaptation, compliance engineering, or another compatibility layer. The model does not distinguish among these technologies. What matters for the mechanism is that the investment is privately chosen, discrete, and tied to a particular additional standard, so its return depends on the set of markets covered by that standard.

This separation between formal and effective compatibility is the source of the institutional feedback. A firm may weaken the product-market boundary created by a regional standard without joining the formal coalition or inducing reciprocal support by coalition members. In the intermediate-cost region, the outsider can profitably support the two-country bloc standard while members still decline to support the outsider's standard. Lower compatibility costs therefore need not have a uniform effect on the attractiveness of formal harmonization: what matters is which firms adopt and which coalition-specific rents their investments remove. The fixed cost selects a continuation state; it does not mechanically rank public regimes.

The same distinction disciplines the ``technological substitute, political complement'' interpretation. Private compatibility is a technological substitute for multilateral harmonization in the narrow sense that it reduces the effective compatibility difference between SU and IS in member markets. Yet selective erosion can raise a member government's relative gain from broader harmonization by removing a payoff component specific to regional exclusion. This is the binary government-incentive comparison established in Corollary~\ref{cor:technological-political}, not a claim of general supermodularity or continuous-action complementarity.

Stability should likewise not be conflated with efficiency. The finding that $\rho^{IS}$ is uniquely stable in the intermediate region means that no admissible coalition strictly blocks it under the maintained continuation payoffs. It does not establish that international standardization maximizes world welfare, and instability of a regional union does not imply that regional harmonization is inefficient. Government preference in the model is a national-welfare comparison used to evaluate strict blocking.

The mechanism also suggests empirical questions without providing empirical validation. One could ask whether declines in the cost of multi-standard production, interoperability investment, or cross-standard certification are associated with weaker dependence on regional exclusivity and changes in government support for broader harmonization. Such patterns would require institution-specific measurement and a separate identification strategy. The present analysis supplies a mechanism linking these objects; it does not establish their prevalence or causal magnitude.

\subsection{Scope and robustness of the mechanism}
\label{subsec:discussion-scope}

The symmetric Main Model is useful because it separates the stability mechanism from partner selection. Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability} requires no market-size asymmetry: $m_1=m_2=m_3=1$ is sufficient for the private-compatibility-induced transition from regional SUs to IS. Section~\ref{sec:secondary} introduces $m_3=1-\delta$ only to rank potential partners in a no-adoption continuation state. Proposition~\ref{prop:market-size-partner-selection} is therefore a partner-ranking result rather than a coalition-stability theorem, and market-size heterogeneity is not the source of selective erosion.

Within the model, four ingredients organize the mechanism. First, an exclusive regional arrangement creates a member-specific continuation advantage in some markets. Second, a private technological response can remove that advantage. Third, some disadvantage associated with remaining outside the broader arrangement survives the response. Fourth, governments compare formal institutions using the continuation payoffs generated after private adaptation. The Cournot microfoundation makes these objects explicit as $\mathscr E_i$ and $\mathscr D_i$. This accounting isolates selective rather than uniform erosion of institutional rents, but the paper does not prove the same ranking reversal under arbitrary oligopoly structures.

Network effects enter the canonical demand system and affect the product-market payoff blocks, but the paper does not claim that they are necessary for selective erosion. Conversely, the analysis does not show that network effects are irrelevant: they remain part of the environment in which the verified open parameter region is constructed. The central claim concerns the asymmetric continuation-payoff effect of private compatibility, not a new network-externality theorem.

Other restrictions delimit the theorem's reach. There are three countries and one firm per country, firms compete in quantities, demand is linear, compatibility adoption is binary, the formal coalition menu is exogenous, and governments maximize national welfare. Stability uses strict blocking in an exclusive-membership partition game. Consent-based accession, farsighted stability, coalition-proofness, bargaining-based formation, and transfers are not analyzed. These restrictions permit the private-adoption stage and all relevant blocking comparisons to be solved exactly; they are not verified robustness results.

The three-country environment is intentionally minimal: it distinguishes fragmentation, exclusive regional harmonization, and universal harmonization while keeping private adoption and coalition blocking tractable. With more countries, coalition size, multiple blocs, overlapping compatibility structures, and accession paths would have to be modeled explicitly. Extending the result to larger coalition networks therefore requires additional coalition-formation structure rather than a mechanical replication of the three-country proof.

Cournot competition serves a related purpose. It yields closed-form quantities and profit blocks that connect compatibility decisions directly to government continuation payoffs. The present theorem does not establish the same stability reversal under differentiated Bertrand competition, heterogeneous firms, endogenous entry, or other market structures. Those environments are natural robustness questions, but they are outside the frozen model.

Finally, the headline comparison is local to the high- and intermediate-$F$ regions identified in Theorem~\ref{thm:formal-coalition-stability}. The paper does not infer monotonicity as $F\rightarrow0$. When compatibility becomes sufficiently cheap, broader multistandarding patterns arise and the continuation states change; the low-$F$ cases are recorded separately in Appendix~\ref{app:low-f}. The result is therefore an existence and mechanism result on a nonempty open product-market region and a nondegenerate interval of compatibility costs, not a global primitive characterization.

\subsection{Limitations and directions for further work}
\label{subsec:discussion-limitations}

Two extensions are especially relevant for institutional applications. First, compatibility is binary and its fixed cost is exogenous. In practice, interoperability can be partial, quality dependent, or built through cumulative investment, and its cost may depend on R\&D, accumulated technical knowledge, certification infrastructure, or regulatory design. Endogenizing the compatibility technology could create feedback from expected institutional outcomes to firms' investment incentives. That is a substantive extension of the present model, which takes $F$ as primitive and endogenizes only the adoption decision.

Second, the model excludes transfers, side payments, and coalition bargaining. Strict blocking therefore depends on each deviator's own national-welfare gain. Allowing transfers could decouple aggregate coalition gains from individual blocking incentives. Relatedly, accession negotiations, endogenous standards design, repeated interaction, dynamic investment, and incomplete information could change the distribution or persistence of coalition-specific rents. These extensions may alter the equilibrium institutional response, but they do not change what is established here: within the maintained model, post-formation private compatibility can selectively erode the rents sustaining a regional standards coalition and thereby change formal coalition stability.
