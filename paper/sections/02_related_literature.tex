\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The paper relates to three literatures that are individually well developed but emphasize different margins: strategic government policy over standards, private compatibility and firm adaptation, and regional versus multilateral cooperation. The contribution here is not a new standards institution, compatibility technology, or coalition solution concept. It is the feedback created when a formal standards arrangement is followed by a strategic private compatibility decision and governments evaluate the resulting continuation payoffs. This section locates that selective-erosion mechanism relative to the closest antecedents.

\subsection{Standards, harmonization, and strategic government choice}
\label{subsec:lit-government-standards}

One literature studies standards as strategic policy instruments in international markets. \citet{gandalShy2001} are especially close to our environment. They analyze government recognition of foreign standards in a three-country setting with conversion costs and network effects and allow countries to form standardization unions. When conversion costs are sufficiently large, two countries can benefit from a union that excludes the third country's standard. Their analysis therefore establishes that compatibility costs and government recognition policy can generate an exclusionary regional standards arrangement. In their model, however, a foreign firm that faces nonrecognition must incur the conversion cost in order to sell in the destination market. The conversion response is tied to the recognition regime rather than selected through a separate, standard-specific fixed-cost adoption game after the formal coalition has been fixed. Our analysis takes the latter margin as endogenous and asks how the induced continuation state feeds back into coalition stability.

Other work clarifies distinct strategic tensions between firms, governments, and international standards. \citet{barrettYang2001} show that redesign costs and network effects can make deviation from an international product standard rational for an incumbent firm and can also weaken a host government's incentive to enforce that standard. \citet{klimenko2009} studies strategic government policies toward technical compatibility under network externalities and evaluates international agreements over compatibility policy. These papers make clear that compatibility can affect both market competition and public policy incentives; our question is narrower, concerning a privately chosen compatibility response after a formal regional arrangement is in place.

A related trade-agreement literature studies how governments can discipline strategic standards and domestic regulation. \citet{bagwellStaiger2001} analyze international disciplines over domestic policies through the market-access logic of trade agreements. \citet{costinot2008} compares national treatment and mutual recognition for product standards in an oligopoly environment, including horizontal compatibility standards. \citet{geng2019} likewise compares national treatment and mutual recognition when countries differ in preferences over consumption externalities. More recently, \citet{grossmanMcCalmanStaiger2021} study regulatory convergence when firms tailor product versions to local markets and tailoring differences generate fixed costs. These contributions focus on the design or efficiency of international regulatory rules. We instead hold a simple menu of formal standards partitions fixed and study how a firm's endogenous technological response changes the national-welfare payoffs attached to those partitions.

The closest literature on the regional-versus-multilateral standards margin is \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} and \citet{kawabataTakarada2021}. \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} develop a three-country Cournot model with national, regional, and multilateral standards and use the core to characterize which standards regimes can be sustained. They also consider fixed costs of setting up production under different standards. \citet{kawabataTakarada2021} add tariffs and distinguish deep free trade agreements from customs unions, asking whether regional harmonization is a building block or stumbling block for multilateral harmonization. These papers establish that regional standards can interact nontrivially with multilateral cooperation. The distinction in the present paper is not the presence of a fixed cost by itself. In the fixed-cost extension of \citet{takaradaEtAl2020}, firms are assumed to supply all markets and incur setup costs when the prevailing government standards require production under multiple standards. Here, support for an additional standard is instead a strategic firm decision. This creates a continuation-state comparison that is absent when multi-standard production is imposed mechanically by the formal regime.

\subsection{Private compatibility and firm adaptation}
\label{subsec:lit-private-compatibility}

A second literature studies compatibility as a private strategic choice. The classic network-competition framework of \citet{katzShapiro1985} shows how compatibility changes demand and competition when consumers value network size. \citet{besenFarrell1994} emphasize that firms themselves may prefer compatible or incompatible competition depending on the strategic environment. Most directly, \citet{farrellSaloner1992} analyze converters that permit otherwise incompatible technologies to interoperate. Their analysis establishes that private devices can partially overcome an incompatibility created by standards or interfaces; private workarounds to incompatibility are therefore not new to this paper.

The distinction between formal compatibility and alternative ways of accessing network benefits is also central to \citet{doganogluWright2006}. They show that multihoming can substitute imperfectly for compatibility and that this technological substitution can alter competition and the social desirability of compatibility. This is an important antecedent for our use of the phrase ``technological substitute.'' Our political-complement interpretation has a different object: it refers only to a government-incentive cross-difference generated when private compatibility removes a regional exclusion rent. It is not a claim that compatibility and formal harmonization are complements in the general game-theoretic sense.

Trade models provide further antecedents for costly firm adaptation across standards. \citet{fischerSerra2000} allow a foreign producer to face a fixed setup cost when producing at multiple standard levels and show how that cost interacts with protectionist standards policy. \citet{schmidtSteingress2022} combine new cross-country standards data with a heterogeneous-firm model in which firms choose whether to adopt harmonized standards despite sunk adoption costs; harmonization reduces country-specific adaptation costs and expands trade. These papers establish both multi-standard production costs and endogenous firm adoption as economically important margins. The present mechanism does not rely on being the first to introduce either. What matters here is that the profitability of an additional-standard investment depends on the current formal partition and that the resulting asymmetric adoption pattern changes the continuation payoffs used by governments in a subsequent coalition-stability comparison.

Recent work on regulatory diversity reinforces the same claim discipline. \citet{parentiVannoorenberghe2025} develop an imperfectly competitive trade model in which firms incur costs when tailoring products to country-specific standards and show that regulatory cooperation can form blocs. \citet{maggiMrazova2024} study fixed costs of regulatory diversity and show that harmonization can arise spontaneously even without a regulatory agreement. These contributions demonstrate that firm-level costs of serving heterogeneous regulatory environments can themselves reshape harmonization incentives. Our result is different in structure: an outsider can selectively adopt the regional bloc standard, thereby eliminating the coalition-specific benefit generated in member markets without inducing reciprocal adoption by the coalition members in the outsider market.

\subsection{Regionalism, regulatory blocs, and coalition formation}
\label{subsec:lit-coalitions}

A third connection is to coalition formation and the regionalism-versus-multilateralism question. The standards literature already contains explicit coalition comparisons. As noted above, \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} use a core concept to compare national, regional, and multilateral standards regimes, while \citet{kawabataTakarada2021} characterize conditions under which deep regional agreements facilitate or impede a multilateral agreement. \citet{parentiVannoorenberghe2025} show that sufficiently dispersed regulatory preferences can support regulatory blocs. The present paper therefore does not claim that regional standards, standards coalitions, or building-block/stumbling-block questions are novel objects.

Nor does the paper propose a new coalition solution concept. Partition-based coalition formation has a long theoretical history; \citet{hartKurz1983} provide a canonical analysis of endogenous coalition structures. We use a deliberately simple exclusive-membership partition game with strict blocking. The economic object of interest is instead the continuation payoff supplied to that coalition game. Formal membership determines a baseline compatibility structure, but firms subsequently decide whether to support additional standards. A coalition is consequently evaluated after product-market behavior and private technological adaptation have responded to the formal arrangement. This timing makes the coalition payoff endogenous to post-formation firm conduct while preserving a conventional blocking logic at the government stage.

This distinction also separates the paper from regulatory-agreement models in which the government's agreement itself determines the applicable standard or recognition rule. In \citet{costinot2008}, \citet{geng2019}, and \citet{grossmanMcCalmanStaiger2021}, the institutional comparison concerns rules such as national treatment, mutual recognition, or coordinated regulation. In our model, private adoption does not amend the formal agreement. The same regional partition can generate different effective compatibility patterns depending on whether the outsider finds it profitable to support the bloc standard. Coalition stability can therefore change even though the institutional menu and the formal membership of each partition are unchanged.

\subsection{Positioning the selective-erosion mechanism}
\label{subsec:lit-positioning}

The closest antecedents thus provide the surrounding pieces. Government standards policy can generate standardization unions and strategic exclusion \citep{gandalShy2001}; regional and multilateral standards can be compared using coalition stability, including in the presence of fixed multi-standard production costs \citep{takaradaEtAl2020}; private compatibility technologies can overcome incompatibility \citep{farrellSaloner1992,doganogluWright2006}; and firms can endogenously adopt harmonized standards or bear costs of regulatory diversity \citep{schmidtSteingress2022,maggiMrazova2024}. Our contribution is the particular feedback among these margins.

A regional standards union initially creates a member-market component of national welfare associated with excluding the outsider while leaving coalition members disadvantaged in the outsider market. The outsider can then choose privately to support the bloc standard. That decision changes product-market competition in the member markets without changing formal coalition membership and without forcing coalition members to support the outsider's standard. The continuation payoff therefore changes asymmetrically: the member-market exclusion component disappears while the reciprocal outsider-market disadvantage remains. Sections~\ref{sec:selective-erosion} and \ref{sec:coalition-stability} show that this selective erosion can reverse government rankings and, on the identified open parameter region, change the strict-blocking stable set from regional standards unions to international standardization.

The paper is therefore positioned around a mechanism rather than a combination of ingredients. We do not claim novelty from combining standards, fixed costs, network effects, governments, or coalition formation. The narrower claim is that strategic private compatibility can endogenously change the continuation value of formal exclusion and thereby change the political incentives that sustain a standards coalition. This is the channel that links the industrial-organization decision of whether to support an additional standard to the institutional question of which formal standards arrangement governments are willing to sustain.
