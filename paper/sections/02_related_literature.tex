\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

The paper relates to three literatures that emphasize different margins: strategic government policy over standards, private compatibility and firm adaptation, and regional versus multilateral cooperation. The contribution here is not a new standards institution, compatibility technology, or coalition solution concept. It is the feedback created when a formal standards arrangement is followed by a strategic private compatibility decision and governments evaluate the resulting continuation payoffs. This section locates that selective-erosion mechanism relative to the closest antecedents.

\subsection{Standards, harmonization, and strategic government choice}
\label{subsec:lit-government-standards}

One literature studies standards as strategic policy instruments in international markets. \citet{gandalShy2001} are especially close to our environment. They analyze government recognition of foreign standards in a three-country setting with conversion costs and network effects and allow countries to form standardization unions. Their analysis establishes that compatibility costs and government recognition policy can generate an exclusionary regional standards arrangement. In their model, however, the conversion burden is tied to the recognition regime rather than selected through a separate, standard-specific fixed-cost adoption game after the formal coalition has been fixed. Our analysis takes that post-formation private margin as endogenous and asks how the induced continuation state feeds back into coalition stability.

Other work clarifies distinct strategic tensions between firms, governments, and international standards. \citet{barrettYang2001} show that redesign costs and network effects can make deviation from an international product standard rational for an incumbent firm and can weaken a host government's incentive to enforce that standard. \citet{klimenko2009} studies strategic government policies toward technical compatibility under network externalities and evaluates international agreements over compatibility policy. In a related two-country setting, \citet{klimenkoInteroperability2009} analyzes government interoperability standards, trade taxes, and costly compatibility-enhancing investment by the foreign firm. These papers establish that compatibility can affect both firm behavior and public policy incentives; our object is the continuation-payoff effect of a privately chosen additional-standard response after a formal regional coalition is already in place.

A related trade-agreement literature studies how governments can discipline strategic standards and domestic regulation. \citet{bagwellStaiger2001} analyze international disciplines over domestic policies through the market-access logic of trade agreements. \citet{costinot2008} compares national treatment and mutual recognition for product standards in an oligopoly environment, including horizontal compatibility standards. \citet{geng2019} likewise compares national treatment and mutual recognition when countries differ in preferences over consumption externalities. More recently, \citet{grossmanMcCalmanStaiger2021} study regulatory convergence when firms tailor product versions to local markets and tailoring differences generate fixed costs. These contributions focus on the design or efficiency of international regulatory rules. We instead hold a simple menu of formal standards partitions fixed and study how a firm's endogenous technological response changes the national-welfare payoffs attached to those partitions.

The closest literature on the regional-versus-multilateral standards margin is \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} and \citet{kawabataTakarada2021}. \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} develop a three-country Cournot model with national, regional, and multilateral standards and use the core to characterize which standards regimes can be sustained. They also consider fixed costs of setting up production under different standards. \citet{kawabataTakarada2021} add tariffs and distinguish deep free trade agreements from customs unions, asking whether regional harmonization is a building block or stumbling block for multilateral harmonization. These papers establish that regional standards can interact nontrivially with multilateral cooperation. The distinction in the present paper is not the presence of a fixed cost by itself. In the fixed-cost extension of \citet{takaradaEtAl2020}, multi-standard production costs follow from the prevailing government standards regime. Here, support for an additional standard is instead a strategic firm decision made after the formal partition is fixed. The same formal regional union can therefore generate a no-bypass or an outsider-only continuation state, which changes its product-market payoff without changing formal membership.

\subsection{Private compatibility and firm adaptation}
\label{subsec:lit-private-compatibility}

A second literature studies compatibility as a private strategic choice. The classic network-competition framework of \citet{katzShapiro1985} shows how compatibility changes demand and competition when consumers value network size, while \citet{besenFarrell1994} emphasize that firms may prefer compatible or incompatible competition depending on the strategic environment. \citet{farrellSaloner1992} analyze converters that permit otherwise incompatible technologies to interoperate, and \citet{doganogluWright2006} show how multihoming can substitute imperfectly for compatibility. These contributions make clear that private workarounds to incompatibility and technological substitutes for formal compatibility are established ideas.

Recent work makes the private compatibility margin even more explicit. \citet{buccellaEtAl2023} study a Cournot duopoly with network consumption externalities in which firms strategically choose whether products are compatible and analyze quasi-fixed compatibility costs, including one-way compatibility outcomes. Thus neither endogenous compatibility nor a fixed cost of achieving it is new to the present paper. In platform markets, \citet{bourreauEtAl2026} study firms' incentives to provide interoperability when consumers may multihome, showing how network effects and platform asymmetry shape the equilibrium interoperability choice. \citet{mottaPeitz2025} instead study strategic denial of vertical interoperability in a dynamic foreclosure setting with data-enabled network effects. These papers establish interoperability as an active firm-level strategic margin in contemporary industrial organization. They do not, however, study a government standards partition whose continuation value and coalition stability are altered by a post-formation private bypass.

Trade models provide further antecedents for costly firm adaptation across standards. \citet{fischerSerra2000} allow a foreign producer to face a fixed setup cost when producing at multiple standard levels and show how that cost interacts with protectionist standards policy. \citet{schmidtSteingress2022} combine cross-country standards data with a heterogeneous-firm model in which firms choose whether to adopt harmonized standards despite sunk adoption costs. These papers establish multi-standard production costs and endogenous standard adoption as economically important margins. The present mechanism instead depends on the current formal partition determining the return to an additional-standard investment and on the resulting asymmetric adoption pattern changing the continuation payoffs used by governments in a subsequent coalition-stability comparison.

Recent work on regulatory diversity reinforces the same claim discipline. \citet{parentiVannoorenberghe2025} develop an imperfectly competitive trade model in which firms incur costs when tailoring products to country-specific standards and show that regulatory cooperation can form blocs. \citet{maggiMrazova2024} study fixed costs of regulatory diversity and show that harmonization can arise spontaneously even without a regulatory agreement. These contributions demonstrate that firm-level costs of serving heterogeneous regulatory environments can reshape harmonization incentives. Our result is different in structure: an outsider can selectively adopt the regional bloc standard, eliminating the coalition-specific benefit generated in member markets without inducing reciprocal adoption by coalition members in the outsider market.

\subsection{Regionalism, standards coalitions, and coalition formation}
\label{subsec:lit-coalitions}

A third connection is to coalition formation and the regionalism-versus-multilateralism question. The standards literature already contains explicit coalition comparisons. As noted above, \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} use a core concept to compare national, regional, and multilateral standards regimes, while \citet{kawabataTakarada2021} characterize conditions under which deep regional agreements facilitate or impede a multilateral agreement. \citet{parentiVannoorenberghe2025} show that sufficiently dispersed regulatory preferences can support regulatory blocs. The present paper therefore does not claim that regional standards, standards coalitions, or building-block/stumbling-block questions are novel objects.

There is also a distinct network-industry literature in which firms themselves form standards coalitions. In an especially close working-paper antecedent, \citet{economidesSkrzypacz2003} let firms choose affiliation to technical standards coalitions before oligopoly competition and characterize how network effects shape equilibrium coalition sizes. That environment endogenizes the standards coalition on the firm side. Our formal coalition is instead a government-level partition chosen prior to the private-adoption stage. Firms can subsequently support additional standards without changing that formal partition. The two models therefore share the idea that compatibility choices alter product-market competition, but the institutional object here is the feedback from post-formation private support to the continuation value and stability of a government standards coalition.

Nor does the paper propose a new coalition solution concept. Partition-based coalition formation has a long theoretical history; \citet{hartKurz1983} provide a canonical analysis of endogenous coalition structures. We use a deliberately simple exclusive-membership partition game with strict blocking. The economic object of interest is the continuation payoff supplied to that coalition game. Formal membership determines a baseline compatibility structure, but firms subsequently decide whether to support additional standards. A coalition is consequently evaluated after product-market behavior and private technological adaptation have responded to the formal arrangement.

Interoperability and coalition formation also appear together outside the standards-trade literature. \citet{guoLiuNault2024} study coalition formation for critical IT infrastructure when members enjoy superior interoperability and the coalition subsequently chooses resource investment. Their model makes interoperability economically important to coalition membership, but it does not feature a firm-level post-formation bypass that changes effective compatibility while leaving formal coalition membership fixed. This comparison further narrows the present claim: interoperability and coalition formation themselves are antecedents; the object here is the continuation-payoff feedback generated by strategic private circumvention of a pre-existing standards boundary.

This distinction also separates the paper from regulatory-agreement models in which the government's agreement itself determines the applicable standard or recognition rule. In \citet{costinot2008}, \citet{geng2019}, and \citet{grossmanMcCalmanStaiger2021}, the institutional comparison concerns rules such as national treatment, mutual recognition, or coordinated regulation. In our model, private adoption does not amend the formal agreement. The same regional partition can generate different effective compatibility patterns depending on whether the outsider finds it profitable to support the bloc standard. Coalition stability can therefore change even though the institutional menu and formal membership are unchanged.

\subsection{Positioning the selective-erosion mechanism}
\label{subsec:lit-positioning}

The closest antecedents thus provide the surrounding pieces. Government standards policy can generate standardization unions and strategic exclusion \citep{gandalShy2001}; regional and multilateral standards can be compared using coalition stability, including with fixed multi-standard production costs \citep{takaradaEtAl2020}; firms can choose compatibility or standards-coalition affiliation \citep{buccellaEtAl2023,economidesSkrzypacz2003}; private interoperability and multihoming affect product-market competition \citep{doganogluWright2006,bourreauEtAl2026,mottaPeitz2025}; and firms can endogenously adopt harmonized standards or bear costs of regulatory diversity \citep{schmidtSteingress2022,maggiMrazova2024}. Our contribution is the particular feedback among these margins.

A regional standards union initially creates a member-market component of national welfare associated with excluding the outsider while leaving coalition members disadvantaged in the outsider market. The outsider can then choose privately to support the bloc standard. That decision changes product-market competition in member markets without changing formal coalition membership and without forcing coalition members to support the outsider's standard. The continuation payoff therefore changes asymmetrically: the member-market exclusion component disappears while the reciprocal outsider-market disadvantage remains. Sections~\ref{sec:selective-erosion} and \ref{sec:coalition-stability} show that this selective erosion can reverse government rankings and, on the identified open parameter region, change the strict-blocking stable set from regional standards unions to international standardization.

The paper is therefore positioned around a mechanism rather than a combination of ingredients. The narrower claim is that strategic private compatibility can endogenously change the continuation value of formal exclusion and thereby change the political incentives that sustain a standards coalition. This is the channel that links the industrial-organization decision of whether to support an additional standard to the institutional question of which formal standards arrangement governments are willing to sustain.
