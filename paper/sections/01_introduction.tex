\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Formal standards determine which products are designed to work together and which firms can serve a market without costly adaptation. Yet firms need not remain passive when governments choose incompatible standards. They can redesign products, support additional technical specifications, obtain additional certification, or otherwise make their products compatible with more than one formal standard. This private ability creates a basic tension in the economics of harmonization. If firms can cheaply overcome formal incompatibility themselves, public harmonization appears technologically less important. But the same private response may also change the rents that governments obtain from maintaining an exclusive standards arrangement. Private compatibility can therefore weaken the technological boundary created by formal standards while changing the political incentives that sustain that boundary.

This distinction between formal and effective compatibility is economically important because a standards agreement has two conceptually separate consequences. It assigns institutional membership, but it also creates a product-market boundary that affects who competes on equal technological terms. A private workaround can relax the second consequence without changing the first. For example, supporting an additional technical specification can allow an outsider to compete as if it were compatible in the markets covered by that specification, even though its government has not joined the standards coalition. The formal coalition may therefore survive as an institution while losing part of the product-market rent that made membership attractive. Treating formal membership and effective compatibility as the same object would rule out precisely this feedback.

This paper asks how firms' ability to circumvent standards incompatibility privately affects the stability of formal standards coalitions. We show that private compatibility can destabilize an exclusive regional standards union through a selective-erosion mechanism. A regional coalition can initially benefit its members by excluding the outsider from the compatibility group in member markets, even though coalition members remain disadvantaged when they sell into the outsider market. If the outsider subsequently finds it profitable to support the bloc standard, its private adoption eliminates the members' coalition-specific advantage in the member markets. The members, however, have not adopted the outsider's standard, so their reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market remains. Formal membership has not changed, but the continuation payoff attached to the formal coalition has. This asymmetric change can reverse members' ranking between regional and international standardization and can thereby change which formal partition is stable.

We develop this argument in a three-country model that separates formal institutional choice from private technological adaptation. Each country has one firm, and the firms compete in quantities in three segmented national markets. Governments can maintain separate national standards, form one of three two-country regional standardization unions, or adopt a common international standard. The formal partition determines firms' baseline compatibility relations and the standards they natively support. After that partition is fixed, firms decide whether to pay a standard-specific fixed cost to support an additional formal standard. A single adoption covers every market governed by that standard, so the return to private compatibility depends on the geographic scope of the formal coalition. Conditional on the resulting support pattern, firms compete in Cournot product markets with compatibility effects and an adaptation cost for supplying a market whose local standard they do not support. Governments then evaluate national welfare---domestic consumer surplus plus the worldwide profit of the domestic firm---and the induced continuation payoffs determine strict coalition blocking.

The mechanism can be summarized by decomposing a regional coalition member's payoff relative to international standardization. Without private bypass, the difference is
\begin{equation}
    \mathscr E_i-\mathscr D_i,
    \label{eq:introduction-selective-erosion}
\end{equation}
where $\mathscr E_i$ is the member's net exclusion surplus generated in coalition-member markets and $\mathscr D_i$ is its reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market. These objects include the relevant national-welfare consequences rather than producer rents alone. When the outsider privately adopts the bloc standard, competition in the coalition markets becomes the complete-compatibility configuration. The member-market component $\mathscr E_i$ therefore disappears. Because coalition members do not reciprocally adopt the outsider's standard, the outsider-market component $\mathscr D_i$ survives. The key transformation is consequently
\[
    \mathscr E_i-\mathscr D_i
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    -\mathscr D_i.
\]
If $\mathscr E_i>\mathscr D_i>0$, the member prefers the regional union before bypass but international standardization after bypass. Private adaptation changes the political value of formal exclusion without directly changing the formal boundary itself.

The direction of the political effect is not obvious from the technological effect alone. Making the outsider compatible in regional member markets narrows the product-market difference between the regional and international regimes, which might appear to reduce the need for broader formal harmonization. But the regional arrangement was valuable to its members partly because the outsider was excluded from those markets. Once the outsider circumvents that exclusion, the coalition members lose this advantage while still facing an incompatible outsider market. Thus a technology that makes one formal boundary less consequential can reduce the attractiveness of keeping that boundary. This is the sense in which technological substitution can coexist with a stronger relative government incentive for multilateralization.

This decomposition also clarifies the role of the private compatibility cost. The fixed cost does not enter the mechanism as an exogenous welfare wedge that mechanically favors one formal regime. It determines which private-adoption continuation state firms select. In a regional standards union, an outsider's investment in the bloc standard can be valuable in two member markets, whereas a coalition member's investment in the outsider standard affects only the outsider market. The private-adoption game therefore admits a nondegenerate range in which the outsider adopts the bloc standard but neither member reciprocates. In that range, the directional nature of private compatibility is endogenous: the same formal regional partition generates complete compatibility in member markets while leaving the reciprocal incompatibility in the outsider market in place.

Our headline result maps this firm-level response into formal coalition stability. In the symmetric main model, we identify a nonempty open set of product-market parameters for which regional standardization is attractive to coalition members when private circumvention is inactive. When the fixed compatibility cost is high, $F>2T_A$, no firm privately supports an additional standard under either a regional union or separate national standards. The strict-blocking stable set then consists of all three symmetric regional standards unions. The result does not select a unique regional partner: symmetry makes the three two-country unions equivalent.

When the fixed cost instead lies in the intermediate interval $F_L<F<2T_A$, each regional outsider alone finds it profitable to support the bloc standard, while the members do not support the outsider standard and private adoption remains inactive under separate national standards. Selective erosion then makes each regional member strictly prefer international standardization to the continuation outcome under its current regional union. The outsider also strictly prefers international standardization. The grand coalition consequently blocks every regional standards union, while no coalition blocks the international standard. International standardization is therefore uniquely stable under the paper's maintained strict-blocking concept. The transition occurs on an open set of product-market parameters and a nondegenerate interval of compatibility costs; it is not driven by a threshold equality. We do not claim that the stable set varies monotonically with $F$ outside this comparison, because still lower compatibility costs can generate broader multistandarding patterns.

The fixed-cost comparison is useful because it changes private conduct while leaving the formal institutional menu and the underlying product-market primitives unchanged. It therefore isolates a channel from the feasibility of private adaptation to the stability of public cooperation. At high adoption costs, formal exclusion has economic bite because firms leave the assigned standards structure intact. At intermediate costs, private circumvention becomes profitable only on one side of a regional boundary. The resulting change in the stable set is driven by endogenous product-market continuation payoffs rather than by an exogenous change in government preferences or by assuming that countries directly become more favorable to international cooperation.

A secondary result separates this stability mechanism from partner selection. The main theorem imposes equal market sizes, so market-size heterogeneity is not needed for the private-compatibility-induced stability reversal. We then allow two countries to have equal larger markets and the third to have a smaller market, while holding the remaining primitives fixed. Conditional on no private adoption, a larger country prefers the other larger country as its regional partner because replacing a smaller partner with a larger one shifts market mass from the outsider-profit block to the coalition-member profit block. This result ranks potential partners; it is not a coalition-stability theorem and does not imply a uniquely stable regional coalition.

The paper makes three related contributions. First, it identifies selective erosion as a mechanism linking private compatibility to government preferences over formal standards institutions. Private compatibility is not modeled as a uniform reduction in all bilateral frictions. Adoption is strategic, standard specific, and costly. Because a bloc standard covers a different set of markets from an outsider's standard, the equilibrium private response can be directional. That directionality matters politically: the outsider can remove the exclusionary benefit that coalition members enjoy inside the bloc without providing reciprocal relief to those members outside it. In the identified parameter region, private compatibility can thus substitute technologically for part of the market-access role of formal harmonization while increasing governments' relative incentive for broader harmonization. The latter statement is a government-incentive comparison, not a general complementarity theorem.

Second, the model supplies an industrial-organization channel from firm conduct to institutional stability. The formal standards regime determines baseline compatibility and marginal adaptation costs; firms then compare the product-market return from supporting an additional standard with its fixed cost. Those decisions change Cournot competition, operating profits, and consumer surplus under the same formal partition. Governments evaluate these endogenous continuation outcomes rather than institutional labels in isolation. The causal chain runs from firms' compatibility choices to product-market competition, then to coalition-specific rents, government incentives, and formal coalition stability. This feedback is the reason the model is not simply a coalition-formation exercise with an exogenous payoff table.

Third, the analysis adds a private-adaptation margin to the regional-versus-multilateral standards question. Regional standards cooperation and multilateral harmonization have already been studied as competing or sequential institutional arrangements. Our result does not rest on claiming a new coalition concept or a new fixed cost. Instead, the same formal regional partition can have different political continuation values depending on whether firms find it profitable to circumvent its technological boundary. The coalition game is therefore evaluated after post-formation firm behavior has responded. This distinction makes it possible for a change in private compatibility costs to change formal coalition stability even though the set of admissible government arrangements is unchanged.

These contributions build on several established literatures. Strategic government choices over standards, recognition, and technical compatibility are studied by \citet{gandalShy2001}, \citet{barrettYang2001}, \citet{klimenko2009}, and \citet{costinot2008}. Most closely for the regionalism question, \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} compare national, regional, and multilateral standards in a three-country Cournot model using coalition stability and also consider fixed costs of producing under multiple standards; \citet{kawabataTakarada2021} study whether regional standards harmonization facilitates or impedes multilateral harmonization. These papers establish both the strategic-policy environment and the relevance of regional standards coalitions. The distinction here is the strategic private adoption stage between the formal partition and the government continuation comparison.

The private-compatibility margin likewise has clear antecedents. \citet{farrellSaloner1992} show how converters can bridge otherwise incompatible technologies, and \citet{doganogluWright2006} show that multihoming can substitute for compatibility in network markets. \citet{fischerSerra2000} analyze fixed setup costs associated with producing at different standard levels, while \citet{schmidtSteingress2022} model firms' endogenous adoption of harmonized standards in the presence of sunk adoption costs. The contribution here is therefore not that firms can privately overcome standards differences or that such adaptation is costly. It is that the profitability of additional-standard support depends on a formal coalition and that the resulting private adoption pattern changes the continuation rents on which government blocking incentives depend.

The paper is also related to the broader economics of regulatory cooperation. \citet{grossmanMcCalmanStaiger2021} study regulatory convergence when product tailoring across markets entails fixed costs. \citet{parentiVannoorenberghe2025} analyze regulatory cooperation and bloc formation under imperfect competition and country-specific standards, and \citet{maggiMrazova2024} show how fixed costs of regulatory diversity can induce spontaneous harmonization even without a regulatory agreement. These results reinforce why our contribution cannot be described as harmonization plus fixed costs. Our narrower object is the selective continuation-payoff change caused by a strategic outsider bypass when the formal regional coalition remains in place. Section~\ref{sec:literature} develops these comparisons in greater detail.

The stability concept itself is deliberately conventional. We represent formal standards regimes as partitions and use strict blocking to evaluate whether a coalition can profitably induce another formal partition, building on the broader coalition-formation tradition exemplified by \citet{hartKurz1983}. The substantive novelty is in the payoff that the coalition game receives from the preceding industrial-organization stages. This also disciplines the interpretation of the result. Stability is not a statement of global efficiency: international standardization being uniquely stable in the intermediate-cost region does not establish that it maximizes world welfare, and instability of a regional union does not imply that regional harmonization is inefficient.
