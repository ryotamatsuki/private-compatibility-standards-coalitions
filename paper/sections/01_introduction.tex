\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Formal standards determine which products are designed to work together and which firms can serve a market without costly adaptation. Yet firms need not remain passive when governments choose incompatible standards. They can redesign products, support additional technical specifications, obtain additional certification, or otherwise make their products compatible with more than one formal standard. This private ability creates a basic tension in the economics of harmonization. If firms can cheaply overcome formal incompatibility themselves, public harmonization appears technologically less important. But the same private response may also change the rents that governments obtain from maintaining an exclusive standards arrangement. Private compatibility can therefore weaken the technological boundary created by formal standards while changing the political incentives that sustain that boundary.

This distinction between formal and effective compatibility is economically important because a standards agreement has two conceptually separate consequences. It assigns institutional membership, but it also creates a product-market boundary that affects who competes on equal technological terms. A private workaround can relax the second consequence without changing the first. Supporting an additional technical specification can allow an outsider to compete as if it were compatible in markets covered by that specification even though its government has not joined the standards coalition. The formal coalition may therefore remain unchanged while losing part of the product-market rent that made it attractive to members.

This paper asks how firms' ability to circumvent standards incompatibility privately affects the stability of formal standards coalitions. We identify a selective-erosion mechanism. A regional coalition can initially benefit its members by excluding the outsider from the compatibility group in member markets, even though coalition members remain disadvantaged when they sell into the outsider market. If the outsider subsequently finds it profitable to support the bloc standard, its private adoption eliminates the members' coalition-specific advantage in member markets. The members, however, have not adopted the outsider's standard, so their reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market remains. Formal membership has not changed, but the continuation payoff attached to the formal coalition has. This asymmetric change can reverse members' ranking between regional and international standardization and thereby change which formal partition is stable.

Strategic private compatibility is itself an established industrial-organization margin. Classic work studies converters and multihoming as ways to overcome incompatibility \citep{farrellSaloner1992,doganogluWright2006}, while more recent work makes compatibility or interoperability an endogenous firm decision in network markets \citep{buccellaEtAl2023,bourreauEtAl2026,mottaPeitz2025}. The contribution here is not the existence of a private compatibility technology or a fixed cost of using it. It is the feedback from a post-coalition private compatibility decision into the continuation rents that sustain a previously chosen formal standards arrangement.

We develop this argument in a three-country model that separates formal institutional choice from private technological adaptation. Each country has one firm, and the firms compete in quantities in three segmented national markets. Governments can maintain separate national standards, form one of three two-country regional standardization unions, or adopt a common international standard. After the formal partition is fixed, firms decide whether to pay a standard-specific fixed cost to support an additional formal standard. A single adoption covers every market governed by that standard, so the return to private compatibility depends on the geographic scope of the formal coalition. Conditional on the resulting support pattern, firms compete in Cournot product markets with compatibility effects and an adaptation cost for supplying a market whose local standard they do not support. Governments evaluate national welfare---domestic consumer surplus plus the worldwide profit of the domestic firm---and the induced continuation payoffs determine strict coalition blocking.

The mechanism can be summarized by decomposing a regional coalition member's payoff relative to international standardization. Without private bypass, the difference is
\begin{equation}
    \mathscr E_i-\mathscr D_i,
    \label{eq:introduction-selective-erosion}
\end{equation}
where $\mathscr E_i$ is the member's net exclusion surplus generated in coalition-member markets and $\mathscr D_i$ is its reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market. These objects include the relevant national-welfare consequences rather than producer rents alone. When the outsider privately adopts the bloc standard, competition in the coalition markets becomes the complete-compatibility configuration. The member-market component $\mathscr E_i$ disappears, whereas $\mathscr D_i$ survives because coalition members do not reciprocally adopt the outsider's standard. The key transformation is therefore
\[
    \mathscr E_i-\mathscr D_i
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    -\mathscr D_i.
\]
If $\mathscr E_i>\mathscr D_i>0$, the member prefers the regional union before bypass but international standardization after bypass. Private adaptation changes the political value of formal exclusion without directly changing the formal boundary itself.

The political effect is not implied by the technological effect alone. Making the outsider compatible in regional member markets narrows the product-market difference between the regional and international regimes, which might seem to reduce the need for broader formal harmonization. But the regional arrangement was valuable to members partly because the outsider was excluded from those markets. Once that exclusion is privately circumvented, members lose this advantage while still facing an incompatible outsider market. A technology that makes one formal boundary less consequential can therefore reduce the attractiveness of keeping that boundary.

The fixed compatibility cost determines when this directional response arises. In a regional standards union, an outsider's investment in the bloc standard can be valuable in two member markets, whereas a coalition member's investment in the outsider standard affects only the outsider market. The private-adoption game consequently admits a nondegenerate range in which the outsider adopts the bloc standard but neither member reciprocates. In that range, the same formal regional partition generates complete compatibility in member markets while reciprocal incompatibility survives in the outsider market. The fixed cost selects the private continuation state; it is not an exogenous welfare wedge that mechanically ranks formal regimes.

Our headline result maps this firm-level response into formal coalition stability. In the symmetric main model, we identify a nonempty open set of product-market parameters for which regional standardization is attractive to coalition members when private circumvention is inactive. When the fixed compatibility cost is high, $F>2T_A$, no firm privately supports an additional standard under either a regional union or separate national standards. The strict-blocking stable set then consists of all three symmetric regional standards unions.

When the fixed cost instead lies in the intermediate interval $F_L<F<2T_A$, each regional outsider alone finds it profitable to support the bloc standard, while the members do not support the outsider standard and private adoption remains inactive under separate national standards. Selective erosion then makes each regional member strictly prefer international standardization to the continuation outcome under its current regional union. The outsider also strictly prefers international standardization. The grand coalition consequently blocks every regional standards union, while no coalition blocks the international standard. International standardization is therefore uniquely stable under the paper's maintained strict-blocking concept. The transition occurs on an open set of product-market parameters and a nondegenerate interval of compatibility costs; it is not driven by a threshold equality. We do not claim that the stable set varies monotonically with $F$ outside this comparison, because still lower compatibility costs can generate broader multistandarding patterns.

A secondary result separates this stability mechanism from partner selection. The main theorem imposes equal market sizes, so market-size heterogeneity is not needed for the private-compatibility-induced stability reversal. We then allow two countries to have equal larger markets and the third to have a smaller market. Conditional on no private adoption, a larger country prefers the other larger country as its regional partner because replacing a smaller partner with a larger one shifts market mass from the outsider-profit block to the coalition-member profit block. This result ranks potential partners; it is not a coalition-stability theorem and does not imply a uniquely stable regional coalition.

The paper makes three related contributions. First, it identifies selective erosion as a mechanism linking private compatibility to government preferences over formal standards institutions. Adoption is strategic, standard specific, and costly, so the equilibrium private response can be directional. The outsider can remove the exclusionary benefit that coalition members enjoy inside the bloc without providing reciprocal relief outside it. In the identified parameter region, private compatibility can thus substitute technologically for part of the market-access role of formal harmonization while increasing governments' relative incentive for broader harmonization. The latter statement is a government-incentive comparison, not a general complementarity theorem.

Second, the model supplies an industrial-organization channel from firm conduct to institutional stability. Formal standards determine baseline compatibility and marginal adaptation costs; firms then compare the product-market return from supporting an additional standard with its fixed cost. Those decisions change Cournot competition, operating profits, and consumer surplus under the same formal partition. Governments evaluate these endogenous continuation outcomes rather than institutional labels in isolation. The causal chain runs from firms' compatibility choices to product-market competition, then to coalition-specific rents, government incentives, and formal coalition stability.

Third, the analysis adds a post-formation private-adaptation margin to the regional-versus-multilateral standards question. Regional standards cooperation and multilateral harmonization have already been studied as competing or sequential institutional arrangements. Our result does not rest on a new coalition concept, a new compatibility technology, or a new fixed cost. Instead, the same formal regional partition can have different political continuation values depending on firms' strategic response after formation.

These contributions build on established standards and compatibility literatures. Strategic government choices over standards, recognition, and technical compatibility are studied by \citet{gandalShy2001}, \citet{barrettYang2001}, \citet{klimenko2009}, and \citet{costinot2008}. Most closely for the regionalism question, \citet{takaradaEtAl2020} compare national, regional, and multilateral standards in a three-country Cournot model using coalition stability and also consider fixed costs of producing under multiple standards; \citet{kawabataTakarada2021} study whether regional standards harmonization facilitates or impedes multilateral harmonization. A related working-paper model lets firms themselves choose affiliation to technical standards coalitions before oligopoly competition \citep{economidesSkrzypacz2003}. These contributions establish that standards coalitions, network effects, and strategic compatibility choices are not new objects. The distinction here is that governments first determine a formal partition and firms can then alter effective compatibility without altering that partition, so the same formal coalition can generate different endogenous continuation payoffs.

The private-compatibility margin likewise has extensive antecedents. \citet{farrellSaloner1992} study converters, \citet{doganogluWright2006} study multihoming, and \citet{buccellaEtAl2023} analyze a Cournot network industry in which compatibility is itself a strategic firm choice and can entail quasi-fixed costs. In platform markets, \citet{bourreauEtAl2026} study endogenous interoperability with consumer multihoming, while \citet{mottaPeitz2025} show how strategic denial of interoperability can alter future competition. \citet{fischerSerra2000} and \citet{schmidtSteingress2022} provide further antecedents for costly multi-standard production and endogenous standard adoption. These papers reinforce the claim discipline of the present analysis: neither private compatibility nor costly adoption is the novelty. The object here is the continuation-payoff feedback generated when such a private response follows a formal standards coalition and subsequently changes government blocking incentives.

The paper also relates to the broader economics of regulatory cooperation. \citet{grossmanMcCalmanStaiger2021} study regulatory convergence when product tailoring across markets entails fixed costs. \citet{parentiVannoorenberghe2025} analyze regulatory cooperation and bloc formation under imperfect competition and country-specific standards, and \citet{maggiMrazova2024} show how fixed costs of regulatory diversity can induce spontaneous harmonization even without a regulatory agreement. These results reinforce why our contribution cannot be described as harmonization plus fixed costs. Our narrower object is the selective continuation-payoff change caused by a strategic outsider bypass when the formal regional coalition remains in place. Section~\ref{sec:literature} develops these comparisons in greater detail.

The stability concept itself is deliberately conventional. We represent formal standards regimes as partitions and use strict blocking to evaluate whether a coalition can profitably induce another formal partition, building on the broader coalition-formation tradition exemplified by \citet{hartKurz1983}. The substantive novelty is in the payoff that the coalition game receives from the preceding industrial-organization stages. Stability is not a statement of global efficiency: international standardization being uniquely stable in the intermediate-cost region does not establish that it maximizes world welfare, and instability of a regional union does not imply that regional harmonization is inefficient.
