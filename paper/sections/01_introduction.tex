\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Private compatibility may reduce the technological importance of formal incompatibility without reducing governments' incentives to harmonize. Firms can redesign products, support additional interfaces, or obtain certifications for another market's standard. These private workarounds alter the competitive rents from an exclusive regional standard, potentially changing its political appeal even when formal membership does not change.

We ask when one-sided private adaptation changes a regional member's preference between regional and international standardization. In our three-country Cournot model, a regional standard protects domestic producers in member markets but leaves them disadvantaged in the outsider market. When the outsider adopts the bloc standard, member-market consumer surplus may rise while domestic producer rents decline; the foreign adopter's profits do not enter member-country welfare. The outsider's unilateral adaptation does not remove members' disadvantage in its market. Whether international harmonization becomes more attractive depends on the net domestic welfare effect, not compatibility alone.

The central object is an equilibrium-derived residual-rent function. Let $d=\lambda c$, with $0\leq\lambda\leq1$, denote the marginal adaptation cost that remains after the outsider adopts the bloc standard and becomes fully compatible in member markets. The regional member's pre-adoption payoff difference relative to international standardization is
\begin{equation}
    W_M^{SU,N}-W^{IS}=\mathscr E-\mathscr D,
    \label{eq:intro-pre-gap}
\end{equation}
where $\mathscr E$ is the net member-market exclusion component and $\mathscr D$ is the reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market. Re-solving the product-market equilibrium after incomplete adaptation gives
\begin{equation}
    W_M^{SU,O}(d)-W^{IS}
    =-\mathscr D+R(d,v),
    \label{eq:intro-post-gap}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
    R(d,v)
    =
    \frac{d\{(5-4v)d+2(1-4v)\}}
         {32(1-v)^2}.
    \label{eq:intro-residual-rent}
\end{equation}
On the canonical network domain, $R(d,v)$ is increasing in $d$. Conditional on $\mathscr E>\mathscr D>0$, the comparison is therefore exact: if $R(d,v)<\mathscr D$, private adaptation reverses the member government's ranking from the regional union to international standardization; if $\mathscr D<R(d,v)<\mathscr E$, the incentive for international standardization becomes stronger but the regional union remains preferred; and if $R(d,v)>\mathscr E$, private adaptation weakens the relative incentive for international standardization. The technological response does not have a sign-invariant political effect.

Complete elimination of adaptation costs is unnecessary. The fixed-cost adoption and government-ranking conditions hold jointly on an open set with $0<\lambda<1$. At
\[
(c,v,\lambda,F)=\left(\frac1{10},\frac6{25},\frac12,\frac9{100}\right),
\]
the outsider strictly adopts the regional standard, members do not reciprocate, SW induces no adoption, and a member's ranking reverses toward international standardization despite positive residual costs.

This result requires endogenous firm behavior. A single additional standard serves both bloc markets, whereas a member adopting the outsider's standard gains access to just one extra compatible market. Explicit fixed-cost thresholds give a selection-free outsider-only adoption state. Asymmetric adoption is established prior art; its role here is to make the subsequent government comparison an equilibrium outcome.

The institutional application is narrower. At complete bypass ($d=0$), regional unions are stable under strict blocking for high adoption costs. At intermediate costs, outsider-only adoption can make international standardization uniquely stable. This illustrates how private adaptation changes the continuation payoffs sustaining a formal coalition.

That conclusion is institution dependent. With symmetric countries, high-cost regional unions cease to be stable under weak/Pareto blocking because a potential deviator can be indifferent; a small market-size asymmetry removes this indifference. The intermediate-cost international outcome instead rests on strict payoff gaps and survives the tested blocking concepts and small asymmetry.

The paper also reports where the political mechanism fails. First, at the zero-network boundary of the canonical model,
\[
W_M^{SU,N}-W^{IS}
=
\frac{c(13c-6)}{32}<0
\qquad (0<c<1/3),
\]
so the regional union is not initially preferred even though one-way private adoption can still occur. Second, a pre-specified alternative that grants singleton firms their own network benefit eliminates the initial regional advantage throughout the old canonical domain while preserving a one-way-adoption witness. Third, in a pre-specified differentiated-demand environment, the initial regional advantage and the positive member-market incentive term fail throughout the audited parameter box under both Cournot and Bertrand competition, even though outsider-only private adoption remains feasible. The portability failure therefore cannot be attributed to price competition alone. These negative results are part of the contribution because they separate the relatively portable adoption logic from the more specification-sensitive government-ranking effect.

Compatibility converters, one-way adoption and fixed-cost scope are familiar \citep{farrellSaloner1992,doganogluWright2006,buccellaEtAl2023}. Government standards unions, regional-versus-multilateral choices and costly adoption of harmonized standards also have extensive antecedents \citep{gandalShy2001,takaradaEtAl2020,kawabataTakarada2021,schmidtSteingress2022,maggiMrazova2024}. We do not claim novelty in these ingredients or in comparing signs of payoff differences. Our contribution is the equilibrium-derived link between post-formation private adaptation, national consumer-surplus and producer-rent changes, and governments' formal-regime preferences, with explicit joint-feasibility and failure boundaries.
