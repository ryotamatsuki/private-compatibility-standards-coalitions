\section{Introduction}\label{sec:short-intro}
Standards may be formally incompatible but privately interoperable. A foreign producer can incur a setup cost to support another country's technical interface or certification, reducing a government's ability to exclude the producer without changing the formal membership of a regional agreement. The institutional response is not obvious: private workarounds raise member-market consumer surplus but also erode domestic producers' competitive rents, while the members' reciprocal disadvantage in an outsider market may persist.

We ask when a privately chosen, \emph{one-way} adaptation changes a regional member government's ranking of regional and international standardization. We distinguish the private adoption equilibrium from the formal institutional outcome. In a three-country Cournot environment, the pre-adaptation regional-minus-international welfare gap is $\mathscr E-\mathscr D$: a net member-market advantage less an outsider-market disadvantage. After outsider adaptation it is $-\mathscr D+R(d,v)$, where $R(d,v)$ is the residual member-market rent derived from market equilibrium. A lower residual per-unit cost can reverse the preference, strengthen the incentive without reversal, or leave the incentive weaker than before. An exact joint witness ensures that the private action generating the political effect really occurs in equilibrium.

Neither interoperability, one-way adoption, costly multistandard production nor regional standards unions are novel \citep{farrellSaloner1992,manentiSomma2008,gandalShy2001,takaradaEtAl2020}. Endogenous firm compatibility may lead to asymmetric outcomes \citep{innocentiMenicucci2021}, and private platform coalitions can sustain partial compatibility \citep{dingKoShen2022}. Work on costly adoption of harmonized standards also considers firm adjustment \citep{schmidtSteingress2022}. An unpublished working paper by Maggi and Mr{\'{a}}zov{\'{a}} (NBER Working Paper 33318, 2024; revised 2026) argues that regulatory harmonization may arise without an agreement and that cooperation may instead preserve efficient diversity. The claimed increment is narrower: an analytically explicit, \emph{equilibrium-feasible} mapping from private post-agreement adaptation to \emph{national} incentives over formal standards regimes, together with meaningful failure boundaries. The model's coalition-stability result is a full-adaptation application, not a theorem about all incomplete-adaptation cases.

\paragraph{Relation to public policy and private standards choice} \citet{fischerSerra2000} and \citet{grossmanMcCalmanStaiger2021} study the costs of serving markets governed by different standards, while \citet{klimenko2009} analyzes public policies and international agreements concerning technical compatibility. \citet{kawabataTakarada2021} examines whether regional standards cooperation impedes wider harmonization. On the firm side, \citet{buccellaEtAl2023} studies endogenous compatibility with network effects, and \citet{parentiVannoorenberghe2025} links costly local product tailoring to the formation of regulatory blocs. We do not equate these environments: regulatory product standards can address consumption externalities, whereas compatibility in our model changes market demand and producer cost. Nor is a different government objective alone sufficient for novelty. The mechanism under examination is the national welfare consequence of a private outsider adopting the bloc's standard after an institutional partition has already been selected; our model solves the firms' continuation rather than postulating that adoption occurs.
