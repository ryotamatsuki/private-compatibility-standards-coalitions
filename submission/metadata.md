## Title
Private Compatibility and the Stability of Standards Coalitions

## Short title
Private Compatibility and Standards Coalitions

## Abstract
We study how firms' private ability to overcome standards incompatibility affects the stability of formal standards coalitions. In a three-country Cournot model, governments choose among separate national standards, regional standardization unions, and international standardization; firms then decide whether to pay a fixed cost to support additional standards. Private compatibility creates a selective-erosion mechanism. Outsider adoption of a regional bloc standard removes coalition members' exclusion advantage in member markets while leaving their reciprocal disadvantage in the outsider market intact. On a nonempty open set of product-market parameters, regional standards unions are stable when private compatibility is sufficiently costly. For an intermediate range of compatibility costs, outsider-only adoption instead makes every regional union strictly blockable by the grand coalition and makes international standardization uniquely stable. A secondary result shows that market-size asymmetry is unnecessary for this stability reversal but can rank potential regional partners. The analysis links endogenous firm compatibility choices and product-market competition to the continuation payoffs that sustain formal standards institutions.
